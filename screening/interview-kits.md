# Interview Kits

Same structure for all three roles: a short phone screen, a practical exercise,
then a final conversation. Do not skip the practical — it is the only stage that
tells you what someone can actually do.

---

# SALES EXECUTIVE

## Stage 1 — Phone screen, 10 minutes

**Do this yourself, on the phone, never over chat.** For this role the phone
call *is* the assessment. You are listening for warmth, clarity, pace, and
whether they take control of the conversation.

Open with: *"Tell me about what you're selling right now."* Then let them talk
for 90 seconds without interrupting. What you learn from how they fill that
silence is worth more than the next five questions.

Then:

1. What was your monthly target, and what did you actually do against it — last
   three months, honestly?
2. Walk me through your last closed deal, start to finish. How many touches?
3. A customer says *"the rate is too high, I'll go with the other one."* What
   exactly do you say?
4. How many calls did you make yesterday?
5. What made you start looking?
6. What are you expecting on fixed, and what were you earning in incentive?

**Score on the call itself, not just the answers:**

| Signal | Good | Concerning |
|---|---|---|
| Energy | Leans in, asks you questions back | Flat, monosyllabic, waits to be led |
| Clarity | You never have to ask them to repeat | You strain to follow |
| Specificity | Names numbers, dates, products unprompted | Everything is "good", "many", "target achieved" |
| Objection handling | Actually handles Q3 in the moment | Explains what they would do in theory |
| Closing instinct | Asks about next steps before you do | Waits passively for you to end the call |

**Pass bar:** you would be comfortable with this person calling a customer of
yours tomorrow.

## Stage 2 — Live roleplay, 20 minutes

Give them one page on your product, 10 minutes to read, then:

> *"I'm a customer who enquired online three days ago and hasn't replied since.
> Call me."*

Play a realistically difficult customer: distracted, price-sensitive, "let me
discuss with my family". Do not make it easy and do not make it impossible.

**Scoring — 50 points**

| Criterion | Pts | What you are looking for |
|---|---|---|
| Opening 30 seconds | 10 | Establishes who they are and why the call matters, without a script drone |
| Discovery | 10 | Asks about budget, timeline, intent before pitching |
| Listening | 5 | Picks up and uses something you said earlier |
| Objection handling | 10 | Addresses the concern rather than repeating the pitch louder |
| The ask | 10 | Actually asks for a commitment — a visit, a meeting, a decision date |
| Composure | 5 | Stays warm when you are difficult |

Under 30 is a no, regardless of the CV.

## Stage 3 — Final conversation, 30 minutes

- Talk through their incentive expectations concretely. Ambiguity here is the
  number one cause of a sales hire quitting in month three.
- Ask what they need from you to hit target. Their answer tells you whether they
  think in systems or in effort.
- Reference check: one previous manager. Ask exactly one question —
  *"Would you hire them again?"* — and listen to the pause.

## Red flags for this specific role

- Cannot produce a single number about their own performance.
- Blames leads, product, or pricing for every past shortfall.
- Talks about "building relationships" but never about closing.
- Refuses the roleplay or treats it as beneath them.
- Their incentive expectation implies deal volumes their CV does not support.

# DIGITAL MARKETING EXECUTIVE

## Stage 1 — Phone screen, 15 minutes

1. Walk me through the last campaign you ran. Channel, budget, what you were
   optimising for.
2. What was your CPL when you started and when you finished? What changed it?
3. How do you know a conversion actually fired correctly? Talk me through what
   you'd check.
4. Something is spending well but generating junk leads. What do you do first?
5. Which of the four areas — paid, SEO, social, email — are you weakest at?

> Q5 matters. A candidate who claims equal excellence at all four is either
> inexperienced or overselling. Honest self-assessment is a positive signal here.

## Stage 2 — Practical exercise

Send this. **Time-box it to 90 minutes and say so** — it is a screening exercise,
not free consulting, and candidates notice the difference.

> **Part A — Audit.** Here is our website and our Instagram. Give us three
> specific things you would change in your first two weeks, and why. One
> paragraph each.
>
> **Part B — Plan.** You have ₹1,00,000 for one month to generate qualified
> leads for `[PRODUCT]`. Give us the channel split, one sample ad (headline +
> primary text), the audience you'd target, and the CPL you'd expect. Explain
> the CPL number.
>
> **Part C — Diagnosis.** *(paste a small table of real or realistic data —
> impressions, clicks, CTR, spend, leads, cost per lead over four weeks, with
> CPL rising and CTR falling.)* What is happening here, and what are the first
> two things you would do?

**Scoring — 60 points**

| Criterion | Pts | Look for |
|---|---|---|
| A: Specificity | 15 | Names actual elements on your site, not generic advice |
| B: Channel logic | 10 | Split is justified by where your buyers are, not habit |
| B: Ad copy | 10 | Would you click it? Does it match your customer? |
| B: CPL realism | 10 | Grounded in stated experience, and they explain the derivation |
| C: Diagnosis | 15 | Correctly identifies creative fatigue / audience saturation and proposes a testable fix |

Part C is the highest-signal item. Anyone who has genuinely managed a budget
recognises that pattern immediately.

## Stage 3 — Final conversation, 30 minutes

- Have them present their exercise and defend two of your challenges to it. You
  are testing whether they hold a position under pressure and whether they can
  change their mind for a good reason.
- Ask: *"What would you need from us in month one?"* Good answers mention access
  — ad accounts, GA4, the CRM, the sales team's feedback. Weak answers mention
  only budget.
- Ask what they would measure in week one to know if anything is broken.

## Red flags for this specific role

- Talks about reach, impressions and engagement rather than leads and cost.
- Cannot explain how conversion tracking works.
- Portfolio is a slide deck of screenshots with no numbers.
- Every past result is a success; no campaign was ever killed.
- Claims deep expertise in all four pillars with 18 months of experience.

# RESEARCH MANAGER

## Stage 1 — Phone screen, 15 minutes

You are listening for one thing above all others: does this person think in
evidence, or in vocabulary? The discipline attracts both, and they sound similar
for the first five minutes.

1. Tell me about a piece of research you designed yourself. Why that method and
   not another one?
2. What did you find, and what did someone do differently because of it?
3. Tell me about a time your research said something the business did not want to
   hear. What happened?
4. How would you check whether a market number someone hands you is trustworthy?
5. What is the last thing you were wrong about?

> **Q3 and Q5 are the real questions.** A researcher who has never delivered
> unwelcome news has either never been listened to or never looked hard. A
> researcher who cannot name something they got wrong is telling you they do not
> check their own work afterwards.

**Score on the call itself:**

| Signal | Good | Concerning |
|---|---|---|
| Precision | Names sample sizes, dates, sources unprompted | Speaks only in "insights", "deep dives", "learnings" |
| Honesty about limits | Volunteers what the study could not tell them | Every study was conclusive |
| Commercial instinct | Frames findings as decisions | Frames findings as topics |
| Curiosity | Asks about our customers and our data before you offer | Asks only about team size and title |

**Pass bar:** at least one answer where you thought *"I didn't know that, and I'd
act on it."*

## Stage 2 — Practical exercise

Send this. **Time-box it to 2 hours and say so.** Senior candidates are the most
likely to walk away from an unbounded take-home, and rightly.

> **Part A — Sizing.** Estimate the annual market in `[CITY]` for `[PRODUCT]`.
> Show your working. State your assumptions explicitly and mark which one, if
> wrong, would change your answer most. Half a page is enough.
>
> **Part B — Design.** We are considering `[a price change / a new location /
> a new segment]`. You have three weeks and `[₹X]`. What research would you run,
> in what order, and what would each stage have to show for you to recommend
> going ahead? Say what you would *not* do and why.
>
> **Part C — Critique.** *(Paste a deliberately flawed one-page research summary
> — a leading survey question, a self-selected sample, a percentage quoted
> without a base, and a correlation described as a cause. I can write you one
> against your real product; just ask.)* What would you not rely on here, and
> what would you do to fix it?
>
> **Part D — The one-pager.** Take your Part A answer and write the version you
> would send the founder. One page. Decision first.

**Scoring — 70 points**

| Criterion | Pts | Look for |
|---|---|---|
| A: Sizing logic | 15 | A defensible chain of reasoning, not a number lifted from a report. The sensitivity call-out is the mark of a real analyst |
| A: Assumption honesty | 10 | States them plainly; does not smuggle in a guess as a fact |
| B: Method sequencing | 15 | Cheap and fast before expensive and slow. Qual to find the question, quant to size it |
| B: Decision thresholds | 10 | Says in advance what result means go and what means stop. Rare, and the strongest signal in Part B |
| C: Critique | 10 | Catches at least three of the four planted flaws. Catching the missing base is the tell |
| D: The one-pager | 10 | Conclusion in the first two lines. Would you forward it as-is? |

Part C is the cheapest high-signal item — it takes them fifteen minutes and it
is nearly impossible to pass on vocabulary alone. Under 40 is a no.

## Stage 3 — Final conversation, 45 minutes

- **Live sizing, 10 minutes, no notice.** Give them a market they have not seen
  and ask them to size it out loud. You are not scoring the number. You are
  scoring whether they decompose the problem, state assumptions as they go, and
  say "I'd want to check this" at the right moments.
- Have them defend two of your challenges to their Part B design. Test whether
  they hold a position for a reason and change it for a better one.
- Ask: *"What would you research first here, in your first month?"* Good answers
  start with our own existing data — lost deals, CRM, ad performance, past
  customers — before proposing new fieldwork. A candidate who opens with a
  ₹5-lakh survey has not understood the job.
- Ask what they would need from us. Access to sales calls and lost-deal contacts
  is the right answer; a budget and a team is the incomplete one.

## Red flags for this specific role

- Every finding confirmed what the business already believed.
- Quotes market numbers confidently with no source and no method.
- Describes process ("conducted 30 IDIs across 4 markets") but never conclusions.
- Portfolio is decks of charts with no recommendation on any page.
- Outsourced every piece of fieldwork and cannot describe how the data was
  actually collected.
- Cannot name a limitation of their own best project.
