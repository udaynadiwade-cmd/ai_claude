The global commercial aircraft backlog crossed 17,000 this year. Seventeen
thousand and thirty-seven as of July. More than twelve years of production
already sold at today's build rates.

Airbus alone is carrying over 1,200 aircraft for India, and expects to hand
over roughly 120 a year for the next decade. Two a week.

Everyone reads that as an order book. I read it as a procurement problem,
because I have sat on the other side of it.

From 1999 to 2003 I was at GE Aircraft Engines, sourcing across Asia. China
first, then Indonesia, Malaysia, India, wherever the capability was. Mostly
MRO parts: the maintenance, repair and overhaul side, feeding engine shops in
the US and Europe. My job was to find suppliers and get them through
qualification.

In those years I spent real time inside HAL and GTRE, with their vendors, and
with the vendor base that serves ISRO. Same machines, same people, same
approval culture. That ecosystem is where India's share of this backlog will
be built, and it is not a mystery to me.

The lesson that stayed was never about price. The approval chain is the
product. A part is not a part until the paperwork says so, and no supplier
gets a shortcut through that, however good the quote.

For years I moved parts out of Asia to the West. Now I move them into India.
So here is what I actually see in that backlog.

It does not get cleared by the people who assemble aircraft. It gets cleared
by the tier twos and tier threes around HAL, GTRE and ISRO who now have to
double capacity on a deadline set in Toulouse or Seattle. And capacity is not
an order book. It is machines, tooling and inspection kit standing on a floor
in Hyderabad or Bengaluru, commissioned and running.

That is a sourcing problem. It is the one I spent twenty years on.

So, plainly, where Befach fits and where it does not.

We do not supply certified flight hardware. I sourced MRO parts for GE, I know
exactly what that paperwork looks like from the inside, and Befach is not in
that chain. Anyone offering you a shortcut there is selling you a future AOG
and a regulator's letter.

What we do is everything behind the part. Cutting tools and tool holders.
Fixtures and jigs. Metrology and test equipment. Cleanroom and shop-floor
consumables. Material handling. Sourced from China and across Asia, landed
cost visible to the rupee before you commit, customs cleared to your door.
The unglamorous list that decides whether your new line is commissioned in
March or in September.

The backlog is twelve years long. The kit to attack it is a twelve-week
problem, and that is where it usually gets lost.

If you are scaling an aerospace line in India and the bottleneck is equipment
rather than orders, that is a conversation I would genuinely enjoy.
