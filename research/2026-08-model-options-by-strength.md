# B2B options, built backwards from your strengths

**Date:** August 2026 · **Status:** options paper
**Strength stack:** sourcing · logistics · e-commerce · marketing · branding ·
**systems (Master Black Belt)**
**Hard requirement:** acquire the customer once, do not re-acquire

---

## Two things in your profile that you are underweighting

### 1. Master Black Belt is not a credential here. It is the operating edge.

Distribution is, mathematically, a **service-level optimisation problem**. The entire
business reduces to one trade-off: *how much inventory do I hold, across how many SKUs,
at how many locations, to hit what fill rate, at what cost?*

That is safety-stock modelling, demand variability, lead-time variance, ABC/XYZ
segmentation, and statistical process control on order-to-delivery cycle time. **It is
a Six Sigma problem in its entirety.**

And almost nobody in Indian distribution runs it that way. The trade operates on
instinct and relationships. **Udaan burned ₹1,055 Cr in a single year substantially on
getting this maths wrong** — assortment too wide, density too thin, service cost per
order above gross margin per order.

A distributor whose founder can actually model fill rate against inventory cost has a
rare and durable edge. **Weight this far more heavily than you have been.**

### 2. Marketing and branding are unusual in B2B distribution — and they are the
private-label unlock

Most distributors cannot market. It is why so few of them ever build an own brand, and
why private label stays a slide in a deck rather than a P&L line.

You have sourcing (to make it), branding (to name and position it) and marketing (to
pull it through the channel). **That combination is the difference between a 5% margin
and a 25% one**, and it is rarer than any category knowledge you are worried about
lacking.

## The mechanism that satisfies "acquire once"

**Vendor Managed Inventory.** The supplier — not the customer — monitors an agreed set
of stock items and refills them to pre-defined min/max levels. The customer shares
consumption and stock data; the vendor decides when and how much to ship. **The buying
decision moves upstream: instead of the customer raising a purchase order, the supplier
watches usage and keeps the shelf full.** `FACT`

Read that against your requirement. Under VMI:

| | |
|---|---|
| Customer acquisition | **Once.** After that there is no repeat sale, only replenishment |
| Reorder | **Automatic**, driven by your model rather than their memory |
| Switching cost | **High** — they have handed you a process, not just an order |
| Revenue | **Recurring and forecastable** |
| Who wins | **Whoever models consumption best** — which is you |

MRO and consumables are the natural fit: *"bearings, seals, filters, belts, and
consumables that many machines burn through predictably."* `FACT` Predictable
consumption is the precondition, and predictable consumption is a modelling problem.

**This is the single best match between a business model and your specific profile that
I have found in this entire conversation.**

## Four options

### Option 1 — Vertical distributor, own brand, VMI retention layer
*Electricals or another chosen category*

Distribute in one vertical; layer private label on the high-repeat SKUs; convert the
best customers to managed replenishment so they stop shopping.

| | |
|---|---|
| Uses | Sourcing ✓ Logistics ✓ E-comm ✓ Marketing ✓ Branding ✓ **Systems ✓** |
| Acquire-once | **Strong**, once VMI is layered on |
| Scale | **Highest** — electricals is ₹8.7 lakh Cr |
| Capital | Heavy — inventory plus receivables |
| Gap | Category expertise, hirable |
| **Verdict** | **The default. Uses everything, biggest prize** |

### Option 2 — Consumables-as-a-service: pure VMI for one business type
*You do not sell products. You sell a stocked shelf.*

Sign a factory, hotel, hospital or facility once. Model their consumption. Own their
entire consumable spend and auto-replenish. Charge on the goods, or a management fee
plus goods.

| | |
|---|---|
| Uses | Sourcing ✓ Logistics ✓ E-comm ✓ **Systems ✓✓** Branding ✗ |
| Acquire-once | **Strongest of the four.** This is the model in its purest form |
| Scale | Moderate — a customer is worth ₹5–50 lakh/yr `ESTIMATE`, so scale needs either many customers or large ones |
| Capital | Lighter than Option 1 — you hold less speculative stock because demand is known |
| Risk | **Moglix and BulkMRO already run VMI in industrial MRO.** Pick a customer type they do not serve well |
| **Verdict** | **Best fit to the stated requirement; smaller ceiling; contested in the obvious segment** |

### Option 3 — Private-label B2B brand house
*Own brands in commodity B2B categories, sold through other people's distribution*

Build 3–5 own brands in unbranded B2B commodities — electrical accessories, fasteners,
consumables, safety goods. Sell through existing distributors, marketplaces and your
own e-commerce. **You never build a route.**

| | |
|---|---|
| Uses | Sourcing ✓✓ Branding ✓✓ Marketing ✓✓ E-comm ✓ Logistics ~ Systems ~ |
| Acquire-once | Weak on customers; **strong on channel partners** |
| Scale | Moderate — brand-building in B2B is slow |
| Capital | **Lightest of the four** — no route, no receivables book |
| Risk | You depend on other people's distribution, and they can drop you |
| **Verdict** | **The lowest-capital way to use your rarest skills.** Worth considering seriously if raising is hard |

### Option 4 — Category marketplace

## Recommendation

**Option 1 as the business, with Option 2 built into it from day one, and Option 3 as
the margin layer.** They are not alternatives — they are three stages of the same
company, and your strength stack covers all three:

1. **Win the customer** with availability and range — marketing and e-commerce
2. **Keep the customer** with managed replenishment — systems, and the reason you never
   re-acquire
3. **Make the money** on own brand — sourcing and branding

Sequenced: distribute first (learn what moves), VMI the top 20% of customers by month
9, private-label the top 20 SKUs by month 12.

**The only genuinely open question remains the category**, and it is now a smaller
question than it feels. Your strength stack works in electricals, auto aftermarket or
artificial jewellery. It does not work in food, data centres or apparel. That is the
decision reduced to three, and the field test settles it.

## One reframe worth sitting with

You have been asking *"which category should we enter?"*

The better question, given this profile, is **"which customer's consumption can we
model better than they can?"**

Because that is what your Six Sigma training actually sells. Not cheaper goods —
**certainty**. A customer who never stocks out and never over-orders will not leave
you for a 2% price difference, and will not need re-acquiring.

**That is the business. The category is just where you point it.**
