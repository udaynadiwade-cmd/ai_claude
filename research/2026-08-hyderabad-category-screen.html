The regional brand arbitrage

Your six parameters describe a coherent and unusually good business. They also rule out more categories than you probably intend — and they are missing the one screen that decides whether any of it sells.

Bottom line

Start with incense, dhoop and pooja consumables. Add home care within 90 days on the same route. Do not touch food, and do not touch plastic disposables.

Your parameters have accidentally described a negative working capital business: buy from regional brands on 30-day terms, sell to kiranas for cash, collect before you pay. That is the reason this can be profitable early — not the margin, the float.

The gap in your screen is brand pull. Parameters 1 and 2 say "regional, not national" — but in categories where the consumer asks for a brand by name, a regional brand simply does not sell, whatever margin you offer the retailer. Every category below is screened on this first.

What your six parameters actually add up to

Read together they are not six preferences. They are one specific business model, and a good one. Here is what each is really doing:

1
Outside of the Hyderabad brands
You want brands with proven demand somewhere else that have no Hyderabad distribution. Their product risk is already retired; only the market-entry risk remains, and that is the risk you are being paid to take.
2
Not national brands
National brands pay 3–6% retailer margin because brand pull does the selling. Regional and emerging brands pay 15–20% because they must buy shelf space. That differential is your entire business. Fact
3
Quality products
Correct, and it is also self-protection. In India the distributor sits inside the liability chain — a bad product is your legal problem, not only your reputational one. See the trouble map below.
4
Offline friendly
Rules out anything that has already been won online, and rules out brands whose economics assume a D2C price. You want products that live on a shelf and are bought without research.
5
High repeat purchase
A gate, not a preference. Route-based distribution only works if the same shop reorders on a predictable cycle. Durables fail this outright, however good the margin looks on the first order.
6
Cash business
The most important one, and the one you may be underrating. Paired with supplier credit it makes working capital negative — you are funded by your own operations. It is also the parameter hardest to hold in the market. See the problem areas.

Name for what you are building: a market-entry distribution platform for regional brands. The brand gets Hyderabad without hiring anyone; the retailer gets 15–20% margin instead of 3–6%; you take the spread for solving a problem neither can solve alone. That is a real value chain position, not an intermediation tax — which matters, because intermediation taxes get disintermediated and genuine positions do not, at least not as fast.

The screen your parameters are missing

In the cold-drinks category, 68% of kiranas report national brands outsell local ones Fact. No margin fixes that. When a consumer walks in and says a brand name, the retailer's margin preference is irrelevant — he loses the sale by substituting.

So the seventh parameter, and it should sit ahead of all six:

Parameter 7 — low brand pull

The consumer must not ask for a brand by name. Test it in one question at any kirana counter: "When someone buys this, do they name a brand or do they just ask for the product?" If they name a brand, the category is closed to you no matter how well it scores on everything else.

This single screen removes packaged beverages, biscuits, most snacks, and premium personal care — and it is why those categories look attractive on your six parameters and would fail in the market.

Problem areas — what actually breaks these businesses

You asked for the problem areas. These are the seven that kill Indian B2B distribution, ranked by how likely each is to hit you specifically.

1. The credit trap — your parameter 6 versus the market

The incumbent distributor gives the kirana 7–15 days. You are proposing cash. The retailer's honest question is "why would I pay you upfront?", and "because we are a platform" is not an answer. The only answer that works is the margin differential — he earns 15–20% on your regional brand against 3–6% on the national one, which is worth far more to him than 10 days of float on a ₹5,000 order.

Say that number out loud on the first visit. If you cannot, do not open the category.

2. Delivery cost against basket size

This is what killed Udaan — ₹4,561 Cr of revenue and a ₹1,055 Cr loss in FY25 Fact. A ₹150–250 drop cost against a ₹3,000 basket at 8% gross margin is structurally negative and no amount of scale repairs it. The fix is not volume; it is basket size and route density, which is why the recommendation below pairs two categories on one route rather than running one.

3. The assortment trap

Every B2B platform is told by retailers that they would order more if there were more SKUs. Acting on it is how Udaan ended up in lifestyle, electronics and general merchandise, and then had to exit all three. Depth on a dense route beats breadth on a thin one. Hold the line at a few hundred SKUs for the first year.

4. Brand disintermediation

You prove Hyderabad works. The brand appoints its own super-stockist and cuts you out. This is the same structural problem the import business has, and it arrives faster here because your success is visible in the brand's own secondary sales data. Mitigations, in order: a written territory exclusivity with a minimum-volume commitment before you invest a rupee in the brand; multi-brand depth per category so no single brand can leave and take the category; and eventually your own label.

5. Expiry and returns

Any category with a shelf life under about nine months turns your warehouse into a write-off queue. Regional brands rarely accept expiry returns from a new distributor, so the loss sits with you. This alone is a strong argument against food at the start.

6. Cash handling

Cash is the right commercial choice and an operational liability: shrinkage, reconciliation, and a salesperson carrying a day's collections. Mitigate with UPI-first collection at the point of delivery and same-day reconciliation. The parameter should be "no credit", not literally "notes and coins".

7. Counterfeit and quality liability

You will be offered cheap stock through grey channels. Buying it once, from a distributor's position inside the liability chain, is how a good business acquires a criminal complaint. Buy direct from the brand, on invoice, always.

The trouble map — where the legal exposure actually is

You said you want a solution that does not put you in trouble. Two of the obvious categories are genuine traps, and one structural choice works strongly in your favour.

Trap 1 — food, if you take title

Distributors and wholesalers need their own FSSAI licence. It is not enough that the manufacturer holds one. Operating without it carries up to six months' imprisonment and a fine up to ₹5 lakh; selling food "not of the nature, substance or quality demanded" carries a penalty up to ₹5 lakh separately. Fact

This does not make food impossible — thousands of licensed distributors operate fine — but it means spices, snacks, pickles and beverages come with a licence, a compliance function, shelf-life liability and recall exposure from day one. That is the wrong load for a first category. Revisit in year two.

Trap 2 — plastic disposables

India's single-use plastic ban covers manufacture, distribution, stocking, sale and use — distribution and stocking are named offences, not just manufacture. Banned items include plastic cups, plates, cutlery, straws, stirrers, and carry bags below the prescribed thickness. Distributor penalties run to ₹10,000 per violation with a further ₹10,000 per day of continued non-compliance. Fact

Paper and compliant alternatives are fine. But a disposables category run by someone not tracking micron thresholds and the banned list is an enforcement action waiting to happen — and the enforcement is local, frequent, and lands on whoever is holding the stock.

Also live, lower severity

Legal Metrology (Packaged Commodities Rules) — every pre-packed item you sell must carry compliant declarations: MRP, net quantity, manufacturer/packer details, consumer care. Non-compliant packs are enforced against whoever sells them, and small regional brands are exactly the ones most likely to get labelling wrong. Check the pack before you take the stock — this is a real, routinely-enforced risk with a five-minute mitigation.

EPR under Plastic Waste Management Rules — the moment you put your own label on anything in plastic packaging you become a "brand owner" and need EPR registration. Not a day-one issue; a definite phase-three issue.

Drugs & Cosmetics Act — soaps and hair oils are cosmetics. The manufacturing licence sits with the brand, but the Act reaches distributors. Manageable, not free.

The structural point in your favour. B2B e-commerce and cash-and-carry wholesale take 100% FDI under the automatic route — and unlike B2C, this includes the inventory-based model, which is barred for B2C e-commerce. Fact If you ever raise foreign capital, staying strictly B2B is worth real money and real freedom of model. The conditions attached to cash-and-carry apply — buyers must hold valid business credentials, and sales to group companies are capped — so collect and store a GST number or trade licence for every retailer you onboard from day one. That is a five-minute habit now and an unwindable mess later.

Where I am contradicting my earlier advice, and why

In the FY25 note I recommended industrial and MRO consumables as the beachhead. That was for befach.com's import business, screened on import intensity, obsolescence and private-label headroom. It fails this screen outright on parameter 6, because industrial buyers pay on 30–60 day terms.

Both conclusions are right for their own question. But it is worth being explicit that these are two different businesses with two incompatible customer types — an importer selling to distributors, and a distributor selling to kiranas. If you intend one company to do both, that is a decision to make deliberately, not to drift into.

Why incense wins, specifically

It is the only category that scores maximum on every axis, and the reasons are structural rather than lucky:

  • Zero brand pull. Consumers ask for agarbatti, not for a brand. Substitution is frictionless — which is fatal if you are a national brand and ideal if you are placing a regional one.
  • Daily consumption. Not weekly, not monthly. The reorder cycle is the tightest of any category on the list, and Hyderabad household consumption is high.
  • No FSSAI, no BIS, no drug licence. The lightest regulatory load of anything you could sell into a kirana. This is the "don't put us in trouble" parameter satisfied almost completely.
  • Supply is next door. The Karnataka and Andhra manufacturing cluster is the country's centre of gravity for this category — regional, non-national, and a short truck ride from Hyderabad. Parameters 1 and 2 are satisfied by geography rather than by search.
  • High value density. Light, non-fragile, no cold chain, long shelf life. Cheap to hold and cheap to move — the opposite of the detergent problem.
  • Margins are the best on the board, because the category is fragmented and every brand is buying shelf space.

And why you pair it with home care rather than running it alone. A pooja-only basket is roughly ₹1,500–3,000 Assumption — too thin to justify a delivery drop. Home care is heavy and lower-margin but high-frequency and high-value-per-order. Run alone, each has a problem; run together on one route, incense supplies the margin and home care supplies the basket. That pairing is the actual recommendation — not either category by itself.

Route economics — why this is profitable early

The reason to run this model is not the gross margin. It is that the route is contribution-positive at low volume and the working capital is negative.

Assumption Every input is mine. Basket size and drop density are the two that matter most and both are measurable in your first month — do not take my numbers, take your own. The structure is the finding: a route that clears its own cost at 12 drops a day, and a cash cycle that releases capital as it grows rather than consuming it.

The number that decides everything

Drops per day. At 12 the route works; at 6 it does not, and no margin improvement rescues it. This is entirely a function of how tightly you cluster your retailers — which is why the launch plan below covers two or three pin codes, not the city. Density is the whole game, and the temptation to spread out is the single most common way this model is lost.

Start small — the first 120 days in Hyderabad

01Weeks 1–3

Census two pin codes, on foot

Pick two adjacent, dense residential pin codes. Walk every kirana. Record: outlet count, which pooja and home care brands are stocked, shelf price, what margin they say they get, who delivers to them and on what terms, and the brand-pull question. This is two people for three weeks and it replaces every market-size estimate in this document with a census. Do not skip this and do not outsource it.

02Weeks 3–6

Sign 3–4 brands with written territory exclusivity

Two pooja, two home care, all from outside Telangana, none national. Negotiate before you have proven anything: territory exclusivity, 30-day terms, and a minimum-volume commitment that lapses if you underperform. Exclusivity is far cheaper to buy now than after you have shown them the market works.

03Weeks 6–14

One route, one van, cash only

150–200 outlets, one salesperson, one delivery route, 60–100 SKUs. Measure four things weekly and nothing else: drops per day, basket value, reorder rate at 30 days, and collection at delivery. Hold the SKU count. Every request for "can you also get me…" is logged, not acted on.

04Weeks 14–18

Decide on evidence, then clone the route

The go condition is reorder rate above 50% at 30 days and 12+ drops per day sustained for four weeks. Hit both and the model is proven and you clone the route into the next pin code — the second route is far cheaper than the first. Miss either and fix that specific thing; do not add categories, cities or headcount to paper over it.

Where the platform comes in — and when. None of the above needs software. Build the ordering app after route one works, and build it to solve the problem you will actually have by then: the salesperson is the bottleneck on order capture, not the retailer's willingness to self-serve. Indian kiranas overwhelmingly do not self-serve on a B2B app in year one; they order to a person and the app records it. Building the app first is the most common and most expensive mistake in this category.

Scalability — honestly assessed

DimensionAssessment
Within HyderabadStrong. Route cloning is close to linear and each new route is cheaper than the last. Warehouse and brand relationships are already paid for.
Adding categoriesStrong, and this is the real compounding. Same route, same retailer, same collection — every added category raises basket value against a fixed drop cost. This is where margin expands.
To other citiesModerate. Brand exclusivity is usually granted per territory, so each new city is a fresh negotiation. Feet on the street do not transfer. Assume city two costs 60–70% of city one, not 20%.
As a pure technology platformWeak — and be clear-eyed about this. This is a distribution business with software in it, not a software business. It scales like logistics: with people, vehicles and working capital. It will not carry a software multiple and should not be pitched as though it will.

The phase-three connection to Befach. Once a route proves which SKUs move, you know exactly what to private-label — and you already own a China sourcing and import capability that most distributors would have to build. Incense, home care and pooja consumables are all straightforwardly private-labelable. That is the point where this stops being a distribution margin and becomes a brand margin, and it is the only path here that reaches durable profitability. Do not start there; but choose today's categories knowing you want to arrive there.

On the pivot itself — half right

You are moving off imports because of scalability and risk. Those are two different claims and they deserve different answers.

On risk, you are right

The import risks are real, largely uninsurable, and mostly outside your control: a customs classification dispute on a single container, FX movement between order and landing, QC failure discovered after clearance, supplier fraud, freight rate spikes, and India–China policy risk sitting over all of it. Each one can take a quarter's profit, and none of them respond to being managed better. Distribution risk is smaller per event and far more within your control — a bad retailer costs you ₹5,000, not a container.

On scalability, the diagnosis is off

Imports do not fail to scale because they are imports. They fail to scale because sourcing is a service — every deal needs a person, so growth is linear in headcount, and each completed deal teaches the customer how to remove you.

Distribution has exactly the same shape. It grows linearly in routes rather than in dealmakers, and the brand can disintermediate you the moment you prove the territory. You are not moving from a linear business to an exponential one. You are moving from one linear business to another with better risk characteristics, faster cash cycles and a much lower cost per mistake.

That is still a good trade. But if the expectation is that B2B e-commerce is inherently more scalable, it is worth correcting now rather than in eighteen months.

The reframe I would actually argue for

Do not exit imports. Subordinate them.

Both businesses have the identical structural problem — the customer or the brand can cut you out once you have proved the value — and both have the identical solution: own the brand.

Which means the import capability is not the thing you are escaping. It is the supply engine for the only version of this that compounds: you import it, you brand it, you sell it through your own routes. A distributor without sourcing has to buy someone else's brand and can be cut out. An importer without distribution has to sell through someone else's shelf and can be cut out. You are one of very few people in Hyderabad who could hold both ends.

So: keep befach.com running — it generates cash and it becomes the sourcing arm. Build routes on other people's regional brands, because that is how you learn which SKUs actually move and it costs you no inventory risk to find out. Then private-label the winners through your existing China capability. Phase three is not a nice-to-have at the end of this note; it is the reason to choose these categories today.

One practical consequence: it raises the weight on private-label headroom in the category screen. Incense, home care and pooja consumables all score maximum there — they are simple to manufacture, unbranded in the consumer's mind, and squarely within what your existing supplier base can make. That is not a coincidence in the ranking; it is the strongest argument for it.

What I need from you

  1. Is this Befach or a new entity? It changes the FDI, GST and liability structure, and whether the import capability is available to phase three.
  2. Capital available for working capital and the first two routes, and over what runway.
  3. Do you have existing kirana or retail relationships in Hyderabad? If yes, that changes the launch pin codes and probably weeks 1–3 entirely.
  4. Any regional brand relationships already? One warm brand introduction is worth a month of cold outreach.
  5. Are you willing to run feet-on-the-street? If the intent is a pure marketplace with no field team, say so now — the model changes fundamentally and most of this note would need rewriting.

The one thing I would do this week regardless of the answers: the pin-code census. Two people, three weeks, and it converts every assumption in this document into measured fact. Nothing I can buy or research substitutes for it.

Where I would argue against myself

  • Incense may be too small. I have not sized it bottom-up and I am not going to pretend otherwise. It scores best on every qualitative axis, but if the category value per outlet is genuinely small, the pairing with home care is doing more work than I have credited. The census answers this in three weeks.
  • Cash-only may simply lose you the best retailers. High-volume kiranas have the most leverage and are the most used to credit. You may find the model works only with mid-tier outlets — which is survivable, but it caps route density, and route density is the whole thesis.
  • Territory exclusivity may not be obtainable from brands worth having. A good regional brand may already have someone circling Hyderabad. If you cannot get exclusivity, the disintermediation clock starts on day one and the model needs private label much earlier than phase three.
  • This is a low-multiple business. Real, defensible, cash-generative — and unlikely to be venture-scale. If the goal is a venture outcome rather than a profitable operating company, this screen is optimising for the wrong thing and we should redo it against a different objective.
