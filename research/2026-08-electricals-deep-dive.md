# Electricals B2B — how the trade actually works, and where it breaks

**Date:** August 2026 · **Status:** category deep-dive
**Follows:** the horizontal survey, which ranked electricals / FMEG first

---

## The chain

Indian electrical goods move through a **three-tier structure**: manufacturer →
redistribution stockist → wholesaler → retailer. `FACT` The same shape as FMCG, with
the same consequence — by the time goods reach the counter, three margins have been
taken and nobody in the chain has visibility past their own layer.

**Margins are thin.** Electrical distributors earn roughly **8–15% gross on the bill,
and about 5% net** after warehouse rent, power and labour. `FACT` For comparison,
consumer electronics distribution runs 10–18% depending on brand tie-ups.

That 5% number should shape everything you do here. **A pure distribution play in
electricals is a 5% business.** Private label is not an upside case in this category —
it is the difference between a business and a job.

## Who buys, and how differently they behave

| Buyer | Buys | Frequency | Ticket | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Electricians** | Repair and small install items | **Daily** | Small | Walks in, needs the exact item now, will pay more for having it |
| **Electrical retailers** | Restock, breadth | Weekly | Medium | Wants range, credit, and fast replacement of what sold |
| **Contractors** | Project quantities | Per project | Large | Price-sensitive, credit-hungry, buys against a bill of materials |
| **Builders / industrial MRO** | Scheduled + emergency | Mixed | Large | Compliance-conscious, wants certification and documentation |

These are four different businesses. **Pick one first.** Electricians and retailers are
where availability pain is highest; contractors are where the volume is but also where
credit risk concentrates.

## Urgency — the strongest thing about this category

The single most useful line I found in the trade literature:

> **"Local electricians buy daily and value two things above price: availability of
> the exact item they need right now, and credit terms."** `TRADE SOURCE`

Read that again in the language of the painkiller test. **Availability and credit —
two of the three things Indian businesses actually pay for — ranked explicitly above
price, by a buyer who purchases daily.**

Almost no other category on the survey has that profile. A kirana restocking biscuits
can wait until tomorrow. An electrician standing at a site with a dead MCB cannot: the
job stops, the customer waits, and the labour cost keeps running. **Stockout has an
immediate, quantifiable cost to the buyer** — which is exactly the condition under
which someone pays a premium and switches supplier.

## The unavailability problem, and why it is structural

Industry research states plainly that the FMEG supply chain **"is quite fragmented,
which makes it difficult to ensure availability of the products in the market in the
desired quality."** `FACT` Three structural reasons:

**1. SKU depth is brutal, and the tail is long.** Wire alone multiplies by core count,
gauge (0.75 / 1.0 / 1.5 / 2.5 / 4 / 6 sq mm and up), insulation type, colour and coil
length. MCBs multiply by pole count, current rating and breaking capacity. A retailer
physically cannot hold the full matrix, so the tail is chronically out of stock —
**and the tail is where the urgent purchases live.** Nobody has an emergency need for
the item everyone stocks.

**2. Three-tier lag.** A retailer's replenishment travels up two layers before it
reaches a factory. Nothing in that chain sees real-time demand, so stockouts persist
for days on exactly the slow-movers that were already scarce.

**3. Capital rationing at the counter.** The retailer's working capital is finite and
he allocates it to fast-movers. The long tail is deliberately under-stocked because
holding it is uneconomic for him. **That is the gap a platform fills** — you carry the
tail centrally so 200 retailers do not each have to.

The trade already recognises this as the differentiator: when products become hard to
source, *"the strength of a retailer's range and procurement network becomes highly
visible, with the best retailers offering options rather than dead ends and suggesting
suitable equivalents."* `TRADE SOURCE` **Substitution intelligence — knowing what is
equivalent to what — is a data product, and it is where your tech capability earns
its place.**

## Counterfeit — a bigger deal here than anywhere else on the survey

Counterfeit cable in India is **causing fires and deaths**, not just lost brand
revenue. Fakes use inferior copper and insulation that cannot carry the rated load,
overheat in normal use, and are a documented contributor to household electrical
accidents. The **ISI/BIS mark itself is being misused** on substandard cable. `FACT`

The strongest evidence of how serious this is: **the brands are spending money on it.**
Havells has deployed an authentication system (Checko) and Polycab has partnered with
ProductSecure specifically to fight counterfeiting in wires and cables. `FACT`

**This is your value proposition and your biggest liability, simultaneously.**

- **As a proposition:** verified, traceable, invoice-backed supply is worth real money
  to a contractor whose name is on the installation. Nobody wants to be the one who
  wired the building that burned.
- **As a liability:** if you distribute a counterfeit cable and a fire follows, you are
  in the chain of supply. Unlike a fake cosmetic, this one has a body count. **Buy
  direct from manufacturer or authorised stockist, on invoice, with batch traceability,
  every single time.** There is no acceptable grey-channel purchase in this category.

## The four things that will actually hurt you

### 1. Credit is the entry ticket, not a negotiation

The trade guidance is explicit: **15–30 day credit to established local electricians is
what converts them into recurring bulk buyers.** `TRADE SOURCE` Contractors expect
longer.

This category cannot be run cash-only. Plan for a receivables book from month one,
underwrite it deliberately, and price it. The upside is that it makes the eventual
embedded-finance layer natural rather than bolted on — but it means **working capital
is the binding constraint on growth from day one**, not year three.

### 2. You are long copper whether you like it or not

Wire and cable inventory is a **commodity position**. Copper moves, and your stock
value moves with it. Fluctuating material prices are a known problem in the trade —
they wreck contractor project budgets `TRADE SOURCE`, and on your side of the counter
they mean a price fall can wipe out the margin on inventory you already hold.

**This risk does not exist in pet supplies, jewellery or auto accessories.** It is the
single most under-appreciated feature of this category and it should change how much
cable you hold and how you price. Consider entering on **switchgear, fittings and
accessories rather than wire** for exactly this reason — same buyer, same urgency, no
commodity exposure.

### 3. Carrying the big brands gives you no edge

I flagged territory lock-in as the main risk. The data says otherwise, and the truth is
more awkward: **Havells has 77 authorised dealers in Delhi, 38 in Mumbai, 74 in
Chandigarh; Finolex has 123 in Pune.** `FACT`

So dealerships are obtainable — territories are not locked. But that also means
**everyone already has them.** You would be dealer number 78, buying at the same price
as 77 others, with nothing to differentiate you.

**Carrying Havells gets you credibility, not margin, and certainly not defensibility.**

### 4. Safety liability sits with the supply chain

Electrical goods are the one category on the survey where a product failure can kill
someone. Certification documentation, batch records and supplier provenance are not
paperwork — they are your defence. Budget for a compliance function from the start.

## So where is the actual edge?

Three places, and none of them is "sell Havells cheaper":

**1. The long tail.** Carry centrally what no retailer can afford to stock. This is the
availability pain, and it is the only thing that makes an electrician change where he
buys. Margins on the tail are also better than on the fast-movers.

**2. Own label on high-repeat, low-brand-pull items.** Switch plates, boxes, PVC
conduit, junction boxes, lugs, tape, cable ties, tools, basic fittings. **Nobody asks
for a brand of junction box.** This is where 5% becomes 25%, and it is where your
China sourcing capability is a weapon rather than a service. Domestic contract
manufacture works equally well and avoids the QCO question entirely.

**3. Substitution intelligence.** Knowing that item A is functionally equivalent to
out-of-stock item B, and surfacing it at the moment of order. This is a genuine
software product, it is defensible because it improves with transaction volume, and it
directly converts your worst moment — a stockout — into a fulfilled order.

## What to test in Hyderabad, and what would kill the thesis

Thirty conversations. Split across electricians, electrical retailers and one or two
contractors. Ask:

1. **"What did you need this month and couldn't get the same day?"** — Name the SKU.
   This is the whole thesis in one question.
2. **"What happens to your job when that item isn't available?"** — Quantify the cost
   of the stockout. If the answer is "I use something else, no problem", the urgency is
   weaker than the literature suggests.
3. **"How do you know a cable is genuine?"** — Tests whether authenticity is a felt
   problem or an industry talking point.
4. **"What credit do you get today, and from whom?"** — Establishes the real entry price.
5. **"Who supplies you now, and what's wrong with them?"** — If the answer is "nothing",
   walk away.

**The thesis dies if:** stockouts turn out to be rare or costless; substitution is
casual so availability does not command a premium; credit terms demanded exceed what
you can fund; or the long tail turns out to be genuinely low-volume rather than
under-served.

**And keep asking the survey's open question:** a ₹8.7 lakh crore market with no funded
B2B platform is either the biggest unclaimed opportunity in Indian B2B or it has a
defect I have not found. Copper exposure, credit intensity and safety liability are my
three best candidates for what that defect might be — and all three are answerable in
the field before you spend anything.
