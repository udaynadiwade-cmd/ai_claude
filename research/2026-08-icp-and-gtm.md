# Who is the customer, and how do we reach them?

**Date:** August 2026 · **Status:** recommendation
**Question:** manufacturing or retail? And what is the go-to-market?

---

## Answer: manufacturing. Not close.

| | Manufacturing buyer | Retail / distribution buyer |
|---|---|---|
| Buys the **same item** again | **Yes — built into how a factory works** | Partly — always chasing new products that sell |
| Knows what they want | **Yes — gives a part number or spec** | **No — guessing what will sell** |
| **Can we guarantee it?** | **Yes — "matches spec" is objective** | **No — "will it sell" is not something anyone can promise** |
| Payment speed | Slow, 45–60 days, but MSME 45-day rule protects us | Faster, more cash |
| Order size | Larger, predictable | Smaller, lumpy, seasonal |
| **Compliance burden** | **Lower** | **Higher** |

Manufacturing wins on five of six. Retail wins only on payment speed.

---

## The two arguments that actually decide it

### 1. The guarantee only works for manufacturing

The guarantee being built is: **"it will clear customs, it will meet Indian rules, and
the landed price will be what we quoted."**

- For a **manufacturer**, that is exactly the promise they need. They already know the
  part. Their only worry is whether it arrives, legally, at the price agreed.
- For a **retailer**, their real question is **"will this sell?"** — and no one can
  guarantee that. We would also inherit endless subjective complaints about colour,
  feel and finish. Those are exactly the disputes Alibaba refuses to cover, and they
  would land on us.

**We can only sell a guarantee on things we can objectively control. Manufacturing
buys those things. Retail does not.**

### 2. The compliance filter strips retail much harder

The plan is to list only products that can legally be imported. Look at what that
removes:

**Consumer finished goods already under QCO:** toys, footwear, furniture, electrical
appliances (**90 categories mandatory from 1 October 2026**), electronics. `FACT`

**That is most of what a retailer wants to buy from China.** A curated, compliance-clean
catalogue would be nearly empty for them.

For industrial components the same filter removes far less, because QCOs concentrate on
finished consumer goods plus steel and chemicals — not on the long tail of machine parts.

**So our own curation step chooses the customer for us.**

## The ICP, stated precisely

> **A manufacturer in Hyderabad with roughly ₹5–50 crore turnover, who already uses
> imported components, but currently buys them from an Indian trader at a markup.**

Every part of that sentence is doing work:

- **Manufacturer** — buys inputs to produce, reorders the same thing
- **₹5–50 crore** — big enough for real repeat volume, small enough to have no import
  department of their own
- **Already uses imported components** — no education needed. They are not deciding
  whether to import; they are already importing, through someone else.
- **Buys from an Indian trader today** — this is the key. There is an existing price to
  beat and an existing SKU with proven demand. We are not creating a need.
- **Hyderabad** — reachable in person, which is how this trade is actually won

### Where they are
Jeedimetla, Balanagar, Nacharam, Patancheru–Bollaram, Auto Nagar, Sanath Nagar.

### What they import `FACT`
Machine spares, pumps, compressors, motors, bearings, pneumatics, CNC and textile
machinery spares (Rapier, Jacquard, Dobby parts), electrical components, LED chips,
packaging machinery parts.

**Note on bearings:** they appear again here, but as **one SKU inside a manufacturer's
basket** — not as a standalone category to build a business on. That is a better
position than the earlier plan, because we sell to the buyer, not to the category.

## Go-to-market

### The opening question — this is the whole trick

Do **not** ask: *"what would you like to import from China?"*
That is vague and invites a wish-list that never converts.

**Ask instead:**

> **"Which parts do you buy from a local trader today that originally came from China —
> and what do you pay for them?"**

This does three things at once:
1. Identifies a **specific SKU with proven, repeated demand**
2. Gives us **the exact price to beat**
3. Confirms they are **already comfortable with imported goods**

### The five steps

1. **Mine the existing ~100 clients first.** Which are manufacturers? Which reordered
   the same item more than once? Free, and warm.
2. **Visit the clusters in person.** Twenty conversations, one question above. This is
   not a phone or email sale.
3. **Quote landed price against their current trader price.** Our advantage is that the
   trader's markup is invisible to them today and we can make it visible.
4. **First order small and guaranteed.** Fixed landed price, we absorb duty variance.
   The point of order one is to prove the promise, not to make money.
5. **Then widen the basket.** Once they trust us on one part, ask what else they buy
   from traders. Same customer, more SKUs — which is the compounding the last eight
   years lacked.

### What to measure from day one
- **Quoted landed cost vs actual landed cost** — decides whether the guarantee is
  affordable
- **How many customers buy the same SKU** — decides whether own-brand is ever possible

---

## What to avoid

- **Do not start with retail** "because payment is faster." Faster payment on a
  business we cannot guarantee is a bad trade.
- **Do not open the catalogue to all categories at once.** Start with the component
  types the first ten customers actually name.
- **Do not sell on price alone.** The trader can drop price. He cannot offer a fixed
  landed cost with customs handled — that is the part he cannot copy.
