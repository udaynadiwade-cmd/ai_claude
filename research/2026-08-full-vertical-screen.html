Nothing new beats bearings. Two new candidates are worth a second look: welding consumables and diagnostic reagents. Everything else confirms the existing shortlist rather than replacing it.

The two worth acting on

Welding consumables — run this field test alongside bearings, not instead of it

$1.8 Bn in 2026 → $3.72 Bn by 2035 at 8.40% CAGR. Stick electrodes hold 34.5% share specifically because SMEs prefer them for low equipment cost and field portability — meaning the buyer base is exactly the fragmented small-fabricator population bearings targets. Construction is the largest end-use at 32.5%, and Maharashtra alone is 18% of the national market, which tells you it clusters around industrial hubs the way bearings demand does.

Why it's a genuine second candidate, not a distraction: electrodes and wire are consumed continuously rather than held as insurance stock the way a bearing is — so the pooling logic is slightly weaker, but the frequency is higher, which may compensate. And BIS certification requirements are tightening procurement standards, which — exactly as with electrical cable — makes verified, traceable supply a real differentiator against grey-market product.

Practical case for running it in parallel: the same warehouse, the same delivery fleet, and very likely the same customer — a fabricator buying bearings for his machines is a plausible buyer of welding consumables for his shop floor. Add three welding-consumable questions to the ten factory visits already planned; it costs nothing extra.

Diagnostic reagents is bigger but harder, and the reason is structural, not just regulatory. At $11–19.5 Bn growing ~11%, it dwarfs bearings on size. But two things work against the pooling thesis specifically: reagents are expiry-dated, so pooled stock is a depreciating asset rather than a stable one — the opposite of the bearings advantage — and the search results note that larger diagnostic operators are already investing in domestic reagent production and diversified supply chains, meaning the biggest, most attractive customers are vertically integrating away from needing you. The opportunity, if any, is smaller labs who cannot do that — a real but narrower slice than the headline number suggests.

What this pass confirms, not just adds

  • Dairy is a false lead. ₹12,730 Bn sounds enormous, but 55–60% of equipment spend sits with large cooperatives buying direct from global OEMs (Della Toffola, INOXPA, Siemens). The addressable fragmented tail is a fraction of the headline number — the same trap as quoting India's total e-commerce market when your actual addressable segment is a sliver of it.
  • Printing confirms the value-density rule from the pooling framework. It is genuinely fragmented — 200+ small ink makers, most paper through unorganised channels — but ink and paper are low value per unit, so pooling gain is weak even where fragmentation is extreme. Value density matters as much as fragmentation.
  • Laundry is a services market wearing a supply-chain costume. The $11 Bn figure describes laundry services, not a B2B supply category — there isn't yet a distinct, measured market for "things sold to laundries" the way there is for bearings or welding consumables. That absence of data is itself a signal: either genuinely white space, or not yet a real category. Field-testable, not desk-researchable.

Recommendation

  1. Do not add a sixth candidate to the shortlist. Bearings still wins outright. Welding consumables is the one addition worth carrying into the field test — same visits, same warehouse, marginal extra cost.
  2. Extend the ten factory visits to ask about welding consumable spend and pain alongside the five bearings questions already planned.
  3. Park diagnostic reagents as a phase-two category once the operating model is proven on bearings — the expiry and cold-chain complexity is a harder problem to solve first, not an easier one.
  4. Drop dairy, printing and laundry from active consideration — not because the desk research says no outright, but because each has a specific structural weakness (integrated buyers, low value density, absent data) that a field visit is unlikely to overturn.
