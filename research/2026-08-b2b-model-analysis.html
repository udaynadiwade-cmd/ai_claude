Research note · B2B commerce, India · August 2026

Where the margin actually sits

Five Indian B2B commerce companies, one fiscal year, a 63-point spread in net margin — and what it means for an import platform deciding what to become.

Bottom line

Do not build a bigger catalogue. Build a narrower one, put your own brand on it, and lend against it.

FY25 filings say the same thing five different ways: in Indian B2B commerce, revenue is close to a vanity metric and the profit sits in credit and private label, not in moving goods. The highest-margin business in the set earns 39.7% net on one-sixteenth the revenue of the largest. The largest profitable operator makes an estimated 57% of its profit from a lending book that is under 5% of its revenue.

For Befach, the structural threat is not competition — it is that every successful transaction teaches your buyer how to replace you. Private label and embedded trade finance are the only two mechanisms that survive that. Catalogue, logistics and QC do not.

Three things this says

1 — Revenue is nearly meaningless here. IndiaMART earns 92% of OfBusiness's absolute profit on 6.2% of its revenue Derived. Inventory-led models book the full value of goods as revenue, so a ₹22,000 Cr topline and a ₹1,388 Cr topline can end up at the same profit. When anyone in this sector quotes GMV or revenue growth at you, that number is not evidence of anything.

2 — Horizontal is dead; vertical survives. Udaan is the purest horizontal play in the set and it lost ₹1,055 Cr even after cutting revenue 20% and exiting whole categories to stem the bleeding. The two profitable operators are each locked to one buyer type and one materials family. This is not a coincidence and it is not a scale problem — Udaan had the scale.

3 — Profitability at scale is thin enough to be fragile. Infra.Market grew revenue 27% and its profit fell 42%. At 1.2% net margin, a working-capital shock or a freight spike wipes out the year. Thin-margin distribution at scale is not a safe destination; it is a treadmill.

The real finding: the commerce is a customer-acquisition channel for the lending book

OfBusiness is the sector's profitability poster child. Decompose it and the picture changes completely. Its NBFC arm Oxyzo reported FY25 PAT of about ₹340 Cr at a 33.4% PAT margin Fact. Working backwards:

Oxyzo revenue = 339 ÷ 0.334 ≈ ₹1,015 Cr
Commerce revenue = 22,241 − 1,015 ≈ ₹21,226 Cr
Commerce PAT = 597 − 340 ≈ ₹257 Cr

Lending → 4.6% of revenue, 57% of profit, 33.4% margin
Commerce → 95.4% of revenue, 43% of profit, 1.2% margin

Derived This is arithmetic on two published figures, not audited segment reporting. Consolidated accounts eliminate intra-group items, so treat it as directional — but the direction is not subtle. A lending book under 5% of revenue produces the majority of the profit.

This is the single most transferable insight in Indian B2B, and it inverts the usual reading. The commerce operation is not the business that happens to have a lending arm. The commerce operation is the origination engine — it acquires SME borrowers at negative cost, and generates the transaction data that lets you underwrite them better than a bank can.

Disconfirming evidence — read this before you act on it

A 33.4% PAT margin on a lending book is a benign-credit-cycle number. Credit costs are the one line that can go from 2% to 8% in three quarters and erase several years of commerce profit. Every Indian NBFC looked excellent in FY25. The model is right; the margin is not a constant.

What this means for Befach specifically

Befach sits in a structurally better position than any company in that table, and has a structural problem none of them have.

The advantage is real

Import is not domestic distribution. In domestic wholesale you compete against an incumbent distributor's price and you fight for 3–8 points. In import you compete against the buyer's own inability to import — which is a different and far more valuable thing to be selling. Landed cost is a ten-variable problem (FOB, freight, insurance, BCD, IGST, AIDC, surcharge, CHA, port, last-mile), and every one of those variables is a place a first-time importer gets hurt. That complexity is the moat, and it is a moat price competition cannot erode.

The market is not the constraint. India imported $131.63 Bn from China in FY2025-26 — about 17% of all merchandise imports, with the bilateral deficit at $112.16 Bn Fact. The first half of calendar 2026 alone ran $79.41 Bn, so it is accelerating, not plateauing.

The problem that decides your business model

Every successful shipment teaches your customer how to remove you. Once a buyer has your factory's name on a packing list, has watched your QC process, and has seen the duty calculation, their rational move on reorder is to go direct. This is why sourcing agencies do not compound — revenue is re-earned every year rather than accumulated, and your best customers churn because you served them well.

Only two mechanisms survive this: a brand the factory cannot sell them, and capital they cannot get elsewhere. Catalogue depth, QC quality, logistics reliability and price transparency are all necessary, and none of them are defensible.

Why finance stacks rather than competes

The reason to add credit is not that it is a better business than commerce. It is that it earns a second margin on a transaction you are already doing, against collateral you already control, using data nobody else has. One illustrative shipment:

Shipment: FOB ₹40.0L · landed ₹50.0L (freight + duty + GST + CHA ≈ 25%)

A. Commerce — managed marketplace @ 9% of landed
revenue 4.50L − cost to serve 2.70L → contribution ₹1.80L

B. Finance — fund ₹35.0L for 90 days
interest @18% 1.55L − cost of funds @11% 0.95L → spread 0.60L
+ processing fee @1.5% 0.53L − credit cost @2% p.a. 0.17L
→ contribution ₹0.96L

A + B on one shipment = ₹2.76L vs ₹1.80L commerce-only = +53%
Capital efficiency : ₹35L × 4.06 turns/yr → 11.1% ROA

Assumption Every input above is mine, not measured — your real take rate, cost to serve and cost of funds will move these materially. The structure is the finding, not the numbers: a second margin on the same shipment, at roughly half the contribution of the commerce leg, on capital that turns four times a year.

And the underwriting edge is genuine. You see the proforma invoice, the bill of lading, the bill of entry, the QC report and the reorder interval. A bank lending to the same SME sees a balance sheet and a GST return. You are underwriting a specific shipment with known collateral and a known sell-through cycle. That is a structurally better risk than the unsecured working-capital loan the same borrower would otherwise take at 24–30%.

Industrial and MRO consumables is the beachhead. Highest repeat rate of any B2B category, buyers are small enough to be fragmented and numerous, nothing goes obsolete, specs are simple enough for remote QC, and — critically — nobody asks for the factory's brand on a box of abrasives or fasteners. It is the category where private label is easiest and where the Grainger and McMaster-Carr playbooks are proven, both of which are fundamentally private-label distributors, not marketplaces.

Two flags on this section

Electronics scores worst despite being the largest import line. Big markets are not good markets: obsolescence destroys inventory-led models, buyers demand named brands, and remote QC on components is unreliable. Do not chase the dollar volume.

Solar needs a policy check before anything else. India's ALMM regime restricts non-listed modules in subsidised and government-linked projects, which materially affects Chinese module imports. Balance-of-system components are less exposed. I have not verified the current ALMM scope for this note — verify before committing anything to solar.

The sequence

Order matters more than selection here. You cannot start with finance — no book, no data, no lender relationship. Each phase produces the asset the next phase requires.

010–6 mo

Narrow, and instrument repeat rate

Cut to one vertical. Stop selling everything to everyone — 2,400 SKUs across all categories is a catalogue, not a position. Then measure the only metric that matters: what share of buyers place a second order within 90 days, and a third within 180. Everything downstream depends on this number and it is the first thing I would want to see.

026–18 mo

Private-label the top 20 SKUs

Take the twenty highest-repeat items and put your mark on them. This converts a 9% take rate into a 30% gross margin and — the actual point — makes the buyer's direct-to-factory route stop working. This is the single highest-leverage move available to you, and it is Infra.Market's real engine.

0312–24 mo

Trade finance — through a partner NBFC first

Co-lending or a direct-assignment arrangement, not your own book and not your own licence. You bring origination and shipment-level underwriting data; they bring the balance sheet and the regulatory perimeter. This tests whether your data actually predicts repayment before you put your own capital at risk.

0424 mo +

Own the book — only if the data has earned it

An NBFC licence is justified by a proven loss curve across at least one full cycle, not by ambition. Oxyzo took roughly five years to become the profit centre. Compressing that timeline is how a good commerce business gets killed by a bad credit book.

Two of those three multipliers are my judgement with no source behind them. The 15–20% SME share is the weakest link and it swings the answer by more than everything else combined.

How to close it properly — and this is worth paying for. Customs bill-of-entry line data (Volza, Zauba, Export Genius) lists actual importer names, HS codes, consignment values and shipment frequency. Cross-referenced against the DGFT IEC registry, that converts this entire estimate from a top-down guess into a bottom-up census: how many Indian firms import your category, at what ticket size, how often, and who they buy from. It also hands you a named, prioritised prospect list as a by-product. This is the highest-return research purchase available to you and it costs a few lakh, not a few crore.

A worked example of why you should distrust market-size headlines

RedSeer sizes India's eB2B market at $90–100 Bn GMV by 2030, growing 40–45% from $5–6 Bn in 2022. Bain & Accel put B2B e-commerce at $350 Bn by 2027, with marketplace GMV at $55 Bn.

These cannot both be describing the same thing, and averaging them would be worse than using either. RedSeer is measuring GMV transacted on eB2B platforms; Bain's $350 Bn is total B2B trade digitally influenced, a far broader definition — while its $55 Bn marketplace figure is actually the more conservative of the two on a like-for-like basis. Whenever you see a B2B market size quoted without its definition, it is not a number, it is a marketing asset.

What I need from you to make this specific

Everything above is the outside view. Six numbers turn it into your plan, and the first one is worth the other five combined.

  1. Cohort repeat rate. Of buyers acquired in any given quarter, what share ordered again within 90 days? Within 180? If you have not instrumented this, instrumenting it is the highest-priority thing in this document.
  2. Is revenue booked gross or net? Full consignment value or your commission? This changes every ratio and is the most common place Indian B2B numbers mislead — including internally.
  3. Do you take title to inventory today, and if so, what is the average days-on-hand and how is it funded?
  4. Customer concentration. What share of value sits with your top 5 and top 20 buyers?
  5. Realised gross margin by category on the last 50 shipments — not list margin, realised after claims, rework, demurrage and FX.
  6. Capital position. What can you deploy into working capital or a credit book, and at what cost of funds?

If the repeat rate is above roughly 40% at 180 days, the private-label-plus-finance path is straightforwardly correct and the only question is sequencing. If it is below 20%, you have a disintermediation problem that is already acute, and the priority changes: fix retention before adding any new business line, because lending into a churning book is how this goes wrong fastest.

Where I would argue against myself

  • Private label is not a guaranteed margin. Infra.Market runs the exact playbook I am recommending and its PAT still fell 42% in FY25 on 27% revenue growth. Brand needs continuous investment and invites direct competition once it works.
  • Asset-light is profitable but may be capped. IndiaMART's 39.7% margin comes with net additions of only about 14,000 paying subscribers across two years. High margin and structural growth ceiling can be the same business, so “just be IndiaMART” is not free either.
  • Credit concentrates risk you currently do not carry. A commerce business with a bad quarter loses a quarter. A lender with a bad vintage loses several years. The upside case and the ruin case both live in the same line item.
  • ONDC is a real but slow-moving variable. Its B2B layer is early and its headline transaction counts are dominated by low-value B2C categories. I would not build a strategy on it, and I would not ignore it either — watch it, do not bet on it.
