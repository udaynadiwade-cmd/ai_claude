## The finding that makes most of this decision for you

**India has over 400 product categories under mandatory BIS certification as of
2026.** `FACT` Anything covered by a Quality Control Order **cannot be imported, sold
or distributed in India without valid BIS approval, and customs verifies compliance at
the port.** `FACT`

The QCO net now covers toys (imports since Jan 2021), footwear (QCO 2024), electronics
and IT goods (Compulsory Registration Scheme under MeitY), furniture, air conditioners,
water heaters, washing machines, household electrical appliances and hinges — with a
Transition Facilitation QCO notified 25 June 2026 covering ten of these. `FACT`

Enforcement is live: DRI has seized Chinese toys, cosmetics and shoes worth over
₹6 crore in a single action. `FACT`

**Map that onto CJ's category list and roughly half of their biggest categories are
legally closed to you.** This is not a risk to manage. It is a gate.

It also explains the "compliance hurdles" you hit in the import business. You were
not unlucky. You were operating across a category surface where 400+ regimes apply,
and no importer can master 400 regimes.

## Two problems the screenshots reveal before we even get to categories

**[from screenshots]**

**1. The app is built for the wrong customer.** Wishlist. Heart icons. Cart. Prices in
**US dollars**. Consumer discount framing — "$19.64, $35.28, 44% off". That is a B2C
dropshipping storefront, imported wholesale.

A business buyer needs none of that and cannot use the app without: GST invoice,
quantity-break pricing, MOQ display, ₹ pricing with landed cost, reorder-from-history,
credit terms, and a business account with a GSTIN on file. **A wishlist is a consumer
feature. Businesses do not wish — they reorder.**

**2. The goods themselves are B2C impulse merchandise.** Mini printers with cat ears.
Booty-lifting leggings. Wrist strengtheners. Crystal hair removers. Heated puffer
jackets. These are TikTok-ad products designed for a single impulse purchase by an
individual.

**No Indian business buys these to operate, and very few can resell them at volume.**
This is the deeper mismatch: it is not only that half the catalogue is QCO-blocked, it
is that the surviving half was selected for a completely different buyer.

Those two problems are worse than the compliance issue, because compliance narrows your
options while these two mean the current asset does not serve *any* B2B customer as
built.

## Is there a play with the 2M catalogue?

**Yes — but not the one you are running.**

The catalogue is worth something as a **sourcing research tool** and nothing as a
**storefront**.

**As a storefront it fails on four counts:**
1. Roughly half the biggest categories are QCO-gated and cannot be imported at all
2. CJ pricing is dropship pricing, not container pricing — uncompetitive on landed cost
3. India has effectively no de minimis (₹1,000 courier threshold; BCD + IGST on
   essentially everything), so per-parcel economics do not work
4. It is free to everyone, so it differentiates nothing

**As a research tool it is genuinely useful.** It tells you what exists, roughly what
it costs FOB, and what is moving globally. That is real input into the only question
that matters: **which 200 SKUs do we container-import and put our own name on?**

So: **delete the 2M SKUs from befach.com. Keep the CJ account as an internal sourcing
and trend-discovery tool.** Two million SKUs on a website with no inventory behind them
signals a dropshipping site, not an import partner, and it buries the few hundred
products you actually intend to own.

## Vertical, horizontal, or a combo

You asked directly, so: **vertical — with the horizontal capability internalised, not
sold.**

### Why horizontal branding-for-customers is worse than it looks

I have argued against it before on MOQ, bespoke workload and disintermediation. The
QCO finding adds a fourth argument that is more serious than the first three:

**If you private-label for a customer, across arbitrary categories, you are
underwriting compliance risk on someone else's brand in a regime with 400+ product
categories and port-level enforcement.** One toy order, one appliance, one pair of
shoes without valid BIS — the consignment is seized at the port, and your customer,
who has never heard of a QCO, holds you responsible.

You would be selling a service whose downside is unbounded and whose upside is a
one-time sourcing fee. That is the wrong side of that trade.

### Why vertical is structurally safer

**In one vertical you master one compliance regime instead of four hundred.**

That single sentence is, in my view, the strongest argument for vertical in the Indian
import context, and it is rarely made. Auto accessories: you learn which items are
safety-critical and certified, and that knowledge compounds. Imitation jewellery: you
learn there is no QCO and you are done. Horizontal: you re-learn the regime with every
new customer, forever, and you are wrong occasionally.

### The combo that is actually right

| Layer | Horizontal or vertical | Sold or internal |
|---|---|---|
| Customer relationship | **Vertical** — one customer type | Sold |
| Category assortment | **Vertical** — everything that customer consumes | Sold |
| Own brand | **Vertical** — private label within the category | Sold |
| **Sourcing capability + CJ catalogue** | **Horizontal** | **Internal only** |
| Import, QC, customs, landed cost | **Horizontal** | **Internal only** |

Your sourcing and import capability is real and it is your edge. **The mistake is
selling it as a service. Use it as a weapon.** A vertical distributor with in-house
China sourcing can private-label faster and cheaper than any competitor in that
vertical — that is a durable advantage. A sourcing agent selling the same capability
to anyone who asks has no advantage at all, because the capability walks out of the
door with every completed order.

## Recommendation

1. **Delete the 2M-SKU catalogue from befach.com this month.** Keep the CJ account for
   internal sourcing research.
2. **Test imitation jewellery and automotive accessories in Hyderabad** — the
   three-week, thirty-conversation field test from the previous memo.
3. **Whichever wins, go vertical on it** — own the customer type, carry everything they
   buy, and private-label the top SKUs using your own import capability.
4. **Do not sell sourcing as a service to anyone**, however good the enquiry looks.

The compliance regime has narrowed your options to roughly two. That is not bad news.
Two options you can actually execute beats fifteen you cannot.
