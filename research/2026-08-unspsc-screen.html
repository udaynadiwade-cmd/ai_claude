Research note · UNSPSC taxonomy analysis · August 2026

Reading the taxonomy

The uploaded file is a partial extract of the UN's global product classification standard — 13,605 commodity codes, but only a slice of the full system. Here's what it confirms, what it can't tell us, and one genuinely new candidate it surfaces.

Read this before the findings

The uploaded file covers 13 of the roughly 55 top-level segments in the full UNSPSC standard — segments 10 through 26 only. That range is raw materials, chemicals, and heavy industrial/vehicle machinery. It is missing segment 31 (Manufacturing Components and Supplies) — which in the real UNSPSC is where bearings, fasteners, gaskets, seals and springs actually live — and it is missing everything from segment 27 onward: electronics, medical and lab equipment, IT, office supplies, food, apparel, furniture, and all services categories.

So this file cannot directly validate or refute the bearings recommendation — the category it would confirm isn't in the data. What it can do is stress-test the categories that do fall inside segments 10–26, and it surfaces one real candidate I hadn't scored yet. If you want to screen systematically against the complete standard, the fix is simple: the full UNSPSC file (all ~55 segments, roughly 150,000+ rows across all levels) is publicly downloadable from UNDP — worth getting if you want to keep using this method.

What it confirms strongly

Welding — the taxonomy backs the pooling thesis in detail

The family "Welding and soldering and brazing machinery and accessories and supplies" breaks into three parallel classes: 27 welding machinery codes, 23 accessory codes, 22 supply codes, plus soldering and brazing machinery separately. Fact

That three-way split — machines, accessories, consumables — is exactly the shape of a real distribution category: equipment you might rent or finance, accessories you replace occasionally, and consumables (electrode, wire, flux, tips) you buy constantly. The taxonomy's own structure matches the pooling argument from the last note without me having designed it that way.

Auto components — the depth is genuinely striking

One single family, "Transportation components and systems," contains 389 separate commodity codes — the second-largest non-flower family in the whole file. It breaks down as: braking systems (35), vehicle safety/security (32), navigation (30), drivetrain (23), exhaust/emissions (22), interior systems (22), steering (19), tyres and tubes (14), suspension (14), fuel systems (10), wipers (9), and more. Fact

This is the fitment-complexity problem made visible as raw data. Every one of those 389 codes multiplies further by make, model and year in the real world. It is direct evidence for why auto aftermarket scored lower on execution difficulty than bearings — the category is not just large, it is combinatorially large.

What it rules out — capital equipment looks tempting and isn't

Three families have real depth and would look promising on a naive SKU-count read. All three fail the pooling model for the same reason, and it is worth stating precisely:

The rule this confirms

Pooling works on consumables and wear parts — items replaced on an unpredictable cycle because they fail or run out. It does not work on capital equipment — items bought once and used for years. A high SKU count inside a family tells you the taxonomy is detailed there; it does not tell you the category is a distribution opportunity. This is the single most useful discipline this file adds to the screening method — a fast test for whether depth is real or decorative.

The one new candidate — and it comes with a real caveat

Industrial lubricants and greases

A standalone family — lubricating preparations (35 codes), greases (6), anti-corrosives (8) — sitting inside "Fuels and Fuel Additives and Lubricants." Fact On the pooling checklist it looks strong at first pass: standardised by grade (viscosity, ISO VG, API classification), consumed continuously by every factory and vehicle fleet, genuine downtime cost when a machine runs dry, buy-to-operate by definition.

India lubricants market: 5.77 billion litres in 2026, growing to 6.73 billion litres by 2031 at 3.12% CAGR. Fact Large, and the fragmentation is real — no single operator dominates transportation, warehousing or the end-user mix. Fact

Why this scores lower than welding or bearings despite the size

Brand pull is real here, and that breaks the fungibility assumption the whole model depends on. "Major brands like Indian Oil, Castrol and Gulf Oil have cemented their distribution networks in urban and rural retail outlets, ensuring easy availability." Fact Unlike a bearing — where the ISO number is the whole specification and any compliant part works — machinery buyers often standardise on a specific brand for OEM warranty compliance, and that preference is not something a pooled generic inventory can substitute around.

This is precondition 5 from the quick-commerce screen, restated: the category must not already be well served, and brand-driven categories usually are. Lubricants is the closest thing this file surfaced to a new idea, and it still fails the test that made bearings the winner.

What I would do with this

  1. Get the full UNSPSC file if you want to keep using this method properly. It is a free public download from UNDP, and having segments 27 through 95 would let this same analysis reach electronics, medical, food, and — critically — segment 31, which is where the bearings and fasteners category actually sits and could be checked the same way.
  2. The rankings do not change. Bearings stays first on the evidence gathered so far; welding is confirmed as the strongest second candidate, now with taxonomy-level detail behind it as well as the market sizing from the last note.
  3. Do not add lubricants to the field test on this evidence alone. If you want to test it anyway, the one question that would settle it fast: "If we stocked a generic ISO-VG-68 hydraulic oil at half the price of your usual brand, would you use it?" A hard no from most factories confirms the brand-pull problem and closes the category.
  4. Use the capital-equipment filter going forward. Any category surfaced from here on should pass one question before it is scored at all: is this bought once and used for years, or consumed and reordered? Only the second kind belongs in this model.
