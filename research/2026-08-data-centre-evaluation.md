# Data centre supply — evaluated

**Date:** August 2026 · **Status:** evaluation
**Question:** does a B2B e-commerce platform supplying data centre companies make sense,
or is it crappy?

---

## The answer

**As a platform business for you: crappy. Structurally, not marginally.**

**But the underlying observation is right, and it makes the electricals case stronger
rather than weaker.** Read to the reframe at the end before dismissing it.

---

## Why the platform model fails here

### 1. The buyer count is fatal

India's major data centre operators: **AdaniConneX, NTT DATA, CapitaLand, Colt, Yotta,
ST Telemedia, CtrlS, Nxtra by Airtel** — plus hyperscalers building their own. `FACT`
The EPC layer that actually procures is similarly concentrated: **L&T, Tata Projects,
Sterling & Wilson Data Center, Prasa Infocom.** `FACT`

**That is roughly 40 meaningful accounts in the entire country.**

A platform is a machine for serving many buyers cheaply. With 40 accounts you do not
need a platform — you need four salespeople and a relationship. Everything that makes
B2B e-commerce valuable (self-serve ordering, long-tail assortment, route density,
reorder automation, CAC amortised over thousands of buyers) is worthless at 40
accounts.

**This is the opposite of every category we have screened.** Electricals has hundreds of
thousands of buyers. Data centres have dozens.

### 2. Procurement is stage-gated, not transactional

Data centre builds run through **feasibility → site selection → concept design →
approvals → detailed design → procurement → construction → testing → certification →
handover.** `FACT` Procurement is a *stage*, not a behaviour.

And critically: **major systems — UPS, PDU, chillers, gensets, racks — are sourced
globally, with EPC contractors leveraging their own established vendor networks.**
`FACT`

You would be asking L&T's procurement department to replace a global vendor network,
built over decades, with a Hyderabad startup's website. That is not a sale you win.

### 3. You would enter at the bottom of the food chain

The stack is: operator → EPC → MEP subcontractor → equipment OEM → **sub-vendor**. A new
supplier enters at the bottom, which means the thinnest margins, the longest payment
terms, and no leverage in any negotiation.

### 4. Payment terms are the worst of any option we have looked at

Large infrastructure buyers pay on 60–120 day terms with retention money held against
commissioning. `ESTIMATE` Compare with an electrician who pays in 15–30 days, or a
kirana who pays cash. **The working capital profile is the worst of anything on our
list, and you would be funding L&T's project cycle.**

### 5. It is project revenue, not repeat revenue

You supply during a build, then it goes quiet until the next one. **No reorder cycle,
no cohort behaviour, no compounding.** For an IPO story you need predictable recurring
revenue; project lumpiness is exactly what public markets discount.

### 6. Credentialing keeps you out for years

Empanelment with a Tier-1 EPC requires certifications, insurance, references and a
track record on comparable projects. **A new entrant has none of these**, and the first
one is the hardest to get because everyone wants to see the one before it.

## The reframe — and this is the useful part

**Data centres are not a customer segment. They are a demand driver for the electrical
category.**

A 400 MW data centre build consumes staggering quantities of exactly what I have been
recommending you distribute: **LV switchgear, cable, cable tray and containment,
conduit, busbar, earthing systems, terminations, connectors, lighting, and every
consumable the electrical contractors use on site.**

And the people buying that material are **not** Adani. They are the **MEP and electrical
contractors** working under the EPC — and those contractors are fragmented, local, buy
repeatedly, and have exactly the availability pain the electricals research documented.

So:

> **Do not sell to Adani. Sell to the electrical contractors wiring Adani's data
> centre.**

That is the same electricals business, in the same city, with **₹70,000 Cr of local
construction as a tailwind** — and a contractor segment that grows with every
announcement Telangana makes.

It also gives the electricals thesis something it lacked: **a concentrated, visible,
locally-funded demand surge you can point at when raising capital.** "We distribute
electrical goods in Hyderabad" is an ordinary pitch. "We supply the contractor base
building Telangana's 5 GW of data centre capacity" is a different conversation.

---

## What business models actually work in data centres — and why none is yours

| Model | Works? | Why not you |
|---|---|---|
| Specialist channel partner for one OEM (Vertiv, Schneider, ABB) | Yes, real business | Needs deep technical credentials and an OEM appointment; it is a distribution business, not a platform |
| EPC or MEP subcontractor | Yes | A services and execution business — not your fortes, and heavily people-intensive |
| O&M consumables after commissioning | Yes, recurring | Small tickets, still requires empanelment, tiny relative to the effort |
| **B2B e-commerce platform** | **No** | 40 buyers do not need a platform |

---

## Verdict

**Do not build a data centre supply platform.** The concentration, procurement model,
payment terms and credentialing barriers each independently disqualify it; together
they are not close.

**Do use the data centre boom as the demand thesis underneath electricals.** Same city,
same category, same customer type as already recommended — with a named, funded,
publicly-announced growth driver attached.

One concrete addition to the field test: **include three or four MEP and electrical
contractors working on Hyderabad data centre or large commercial projects** in the
thirty conversations. Ask them the same five questions. If their availability pain is
sharper than the general electrical trade's — and it should be, because project
schedules make stockouts far more expensive — **that is your beachhead within the
beachhead.**
