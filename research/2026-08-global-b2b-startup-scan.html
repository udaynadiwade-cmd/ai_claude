Research note · global B2B startup landscape scan · August 2026

Everyone else's playbook

A survey of what's actually being built and funded in B2B commerce right now — India first, then the US, Europe, China, Southeast Asia, Latin America and Africa. Organised by mechanism, not geography, because the same handful of mechanisms (managed marketplace, embedded credit, sourcing aggregation, vertical SaaS-becomes-commerce) keep reappearing under different names in every region. Companies are evidence for a pattern, not the point of the exercise.

Bottom line

Two things stand out across ~55 companies scanned. First, the mechanism list is short and repeats everywhere — roughly 18 distinct patterns cover nearly everything below, and most of them already exist in India in some form. Second, the more useful signal in this scan is the failure data, not the funding data — inventory-led B2B distribution to informal retail (TradeDepot/Wasoko/Twiga/Sabi/Capiter/Copia/Frubana/Chiper) has produced more distress mergers, layoffs, pivots and liquidations than durable winners, across three continents, in the last two years. The India whitespace list near the bottom is short and specific — that is deliberate; most of what looked like a gap on first pass already has an Indian player once you check for it.

Deep dive: Faire — why it was skipped, and what it actually shows

Correction Faire was left out of the mechanism catalog above on purpose, not by oversight — the brief given to the research agents was to mention already-famous names only in passing and spend the detail budget on less-obvious models, and in synthesising, the passing mentions of Amazon Business / Faire / Grainger / McMaster-Carr / Flexport were cut entirely rather than kept as one-liners. That was a reasonable call for a whitespace scan, but it left Faire under-evidenced for anyone actually trying to evaluate it — worth doing properly on request.

How it actually works

Faire connects independent brands/makers (sellers) to independent retail stores (buyers) and inserts itself as the credit counterparty between them, not just the directory. Fact Retailers get net-60 payment terms (order now, pay in 60 days) and free returns on each brand's opening order — this is the wedge, because it removes the two things that keep a small boutique from trying an unfamiliar brand: upfront cash and unsold-inventory risk. Brands get paid out by Faire within days, regardless of the retailer's 60-day term — Faire fronts the cash and absorbs the collections/credit risk itself. No listing or monthly fees. (Brahmin Solutions, Multisellr)

Monetization is a commission on GMV, and the published rate has moved around: 15% on marketplace orders, with a $10 one-time new-customer fee in North America per Faire's own pricing update, but brand-side accounts describe the practical first-order cost as closer to 25% once new-customer and (for non-North-America brands) referral fees stack, dropping to 15% on reorders. Sources disagree on the exact current structure — treat the specific percentages as approximate, the shape (much higher first-order take, lower repeat-order take, plus add-on fees) as the reliable part. (Faire pricing update, Eightx)

Why it works structurally

Faire is mechanism 1 (managed marketplace) fused with mechanism 6 (embedded B2B credit) from the catalog above — not a new pattern, but a clean, well-evidenced instance of combining them. Three things make the combination work: (a) both sides are genuinely fragmented and don't trust each other by default — thousands of single-founder brands, hundreds of thousands of independent boutiques, no pre-existing credit relationship; (b) net-60 plus free returns directly kills the two real objections a small retailer has to trying a new brand (cash and unsold-inventory risk), which is a sharper wedge than price or selection; (c) the credit-risk absorption is the actual product being sold, monetized through the take rate — the same "financing wrapped around a commerce relationship captures disproportionate margin because it's underwriting a transaction it can already see" logic this series flagged in the Oxyzo/OfBusiness comparison. NDR above 110% confirms the repeat-purchase, acquire-once-monetize-forever dynamic this research series screens for.

The disconfirming evidence

The take rate is extractive enough that it's visibly damaging the supply side. Brand accounts describe net margins compressed to roughly 15% after a 25%-equivalent first-order fee stacks on top of wholesale pricing already discounted to ~50% of MSRP — down from ~40% gross margin at direct keystone wholesale. Faire also restricts brands from pricing differently to absorb the fee. This isn't marginal friction; it's a structurally thin deal for sellers that holds only because the alternative (trade shows, cold outreach) is worse, not because both sides love the economics. (Eightx, Lucky Break Consulting)

Growth was partly a price increase, not just volume. The 73% revenue jump in 2023 came in part from raising the effective take rate 16.5%→19%, against a period when GMV growth was actually decelerating (from ~3x YoY in 2021 to ~2x in 2022, slower still in 2023) and the company cut headcount twice. The "hypergrowth throughout" story doesn't hold — there was a real multi-year trough before the 2024–2025 reacceleration, and part of the recovery is pricing power over a captive seller base, not pure network effects.

The zero-commission alternative failed, which cuts both ways. Tundra, a 0%-commission Faire challenger, shut down in July 2023. That's evidence the take rate isn't optional greed — it likely funds the credit-risk absorption and returns guarantee that make the model work at all. But it also means anyone adapting this mechanism has to solve "who eats the cost of the credit risk" from day one; it can't be priced away by undercutting on commission. Ankorstore, the European equivalent, shows the same pattern from the other direction — it also launched near 0% and has drifted toward ~25%. (Savvy Shopkeeper, Wonnda)

Relevance here

Faire's own numbers are real and the mechanism is sound — but read against this research series, it's evidence for a conclusion already reached, not a reason to revisit it. This series' pearls finished-goods-marketplace note (research/2026-08-pearls-finished-goods-marketplace.md) rejected a national B2B finished-jewellery marketplace for this founder on three grounds: it fights entrenched brands for trust, it very likely reintroduces the credit business explicitly ruled out two turns earlier in that conversation, and national reach breaks "easy to execute" for a first move. Faire is the fullest proof that the first version of this model works at scale — and it works because the platform becomes a lender first and a marketplace second, which is precisely the constraint this founder asked to avoid. It doesn't change the recommendation; it sharpens why the recommendation holds. Structurally closest India analogue for the goods (fashion/home/gift wholesale to boutiques) still runs offline through trade fairs and distributor networks — genuinely open, but not open in a way this founder's stated constraints make it a good first move.

Cautionary tales — the failure data is the more interesting dataset

The pattern underneath the pattern

Nearly every failure above shares one of two root causes: capital intensity without a matching path to unit economics (inventory + logistics + subsidised pricing to informal retail, run for years without profitability discipline), or embedded lending risk that wasn't ring-fenced from the commerce business (TaniHub, eFishery). The survivors and the actually-profitable names in this scan — Zhaogang, VinShop, Linklogis, Cartona, Procol, Zip — either avoid owning inventory, avoid owning the credit risk, or were capitalised by a strategic parent rather than pure growth-stage VC.
