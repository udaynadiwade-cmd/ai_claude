# Pet, apparel, jewellery — and the distinction that decides all three

**Date:** August 2026 · **Status:** category assessment
**Question asked:** is there an opportunity in pet / apparel / artificial jewellery,
is there a real problem, will shops want it, painkiller or vitamin?

---

## The distinction that answers your question

**Pet, apparel and artificial jewellery are product categories. Garage, salon,
clinic and restaurant are customer types.**

Your own strategy — *"end-to-end supplies to one vertical so CAC is less"* — requires
a **customer type**, not a product category. "End-to-end" is meaningless except
relative to a customer. You cannot be end-to-end for jewellery; you can only be
end-to-end for a jewellery *shop*, and the moment you think that way you discover the
shop also buys packaging, display stands, cleaning cloth, billing rolls and lighting.

That reframe matters because of a second distinction, which is the real answer to
painkiller-versus-vitamin:

| | **Buy to resell** | **Buy to operate** |
|---|---|---|
| Examples | Apparel, jewellery, pet food in a pet shop | Oil in a garage, colour in a salon, packaging in a restaurant |
| Demand | Derived — depends on consumers wanting it | Non-discretionary — the business must buy to function |
| Trend risk | High. Someone holds dead stock, and it becomes you | None |
| Reorder | Only if it sold | Guaranteed, on a clock |
| Your role | You add to his inventory risk | You remove an operating headache |
| Painkiller? | **Rarely** | **Usually** |

**All three of your candidates are buy-to-resell.** That is the structural problem. A
shopkeeper's biggest pain is money stuck in stock that will not move. A platform whose
proposition is "buy more stock from us, easily" is not relieving that pain — in the
worst case it is the cause of it.

## How to tell a painkiller from a vitamin in Indian B2B

Three things a business will genuinely pay for:

1. **Availability** — you have it today, his usual supplier does not
2. **Authenticity** — he can trust it is not counterfeit
3. **Working capital** — you fund it, or you let him buy 10 instead of 100

Three things that sound good and are vitamins:

1. **Convenience** — ordering on an app instead of on WhatsApp
2. **Price comparison** — he already knows the price better than you do
3. **Catalogue breadth** — impressive in a pitch deck, irrelevant at the counter

Most Indian B2B platforms sell convenience and die. The survivors sell authenticity,
availability or capital.

## The three categories, assessed

### Artificial jewellery — best of your three

| | |
|---|---|
| TAM | **~$4.98 Bn (2025) → $9.73 Bn by 2032, 10.04% CAGR** `FACT` — roughly ₹42,000 Cr |
| Fragmentation | Very high, both sides |
| Your edge | **Strong.** Most cheap imitation jewellery is Chinese (Yiwu). Your sourcing capability is directly differentiating here — more than in any other category you have named |
| Compliance | **Low.** BIS hallmarking applies to precious metals, not imitation |
| Repeat | High — fashion refresh cycles are short and ticket sizes are small |
| Real pain | *"I have to travel to Rajkot or Mumbai three or four times a year, and between trips I cannot get fresh designs."* That is a genuine availability pain and you can relieve it |
| Against it | Design and trend risk sits with whoever holds stock. Rajkot is an efficient, entrenched hub — and retailers go there for credit and relationship, not only for goods |

**Verdict:** viable, and the one where your China capability matters most. Smaller
prize than auto, faster to start, real painkiller *if* you deliver design freshness at
low MOQ rather than competing with Rajkot on price.

### Pet — good market, wrong channel

| | |
|---|---|
| TAM | Estimates range from ~$1.0 Bn to ~$8.6 Bn depending on the source and what is being counted. **Treat every pet market number you read with suspicion** — the spread here is larger than in any category I have looked at |
| Growth | Fastest of the three, 8–11%, online pet food growing far faster |
| Real problem | Pet *food* margin is captured by Pedigree, Royal Canin and Drools through entrenched distributors. You would be a third wheel on the highest-volume SKUs |
| The killer | **Pet retail is losing share to D2C** — Supertails, Heads Up For Tails, Amazon. Supplying a channel that is itself being disrupted is a bad structural position |

**Verdict:** the category is growing; the *customer* is not. If you want pet, the
buy-to-operate customers are **vet clinics and grooming salons**, not pet shops — but
their count is too small to build an IPO-scale business on.

### Apparel — do not

- **Maximum trend risk**, and apparel dead stock is worth close to nothing
- **SKU explosion** — one design becomes 20–30 SKUs across size and colour
- **Returns culture** — Indian apparel wholesale runs on exchange rights; you inherit that
- **Entrenched hubs with better terms than you can offer** — Surat, Tirupur, Ludhiana,
  Gandhi Nagar, all giving 60–90 day credit on relationships decades old
- **Udaan exited lifestyle.** That is evidence, not opinion

The only variant that works is zero-inventory replenishment — retailer orders five
pieces, you dropship, he carries no dead stock. That *is* a real painkiller. It is also
thin-margin, ops-heavy, and a different company from the one you are describing.

**Verdict: no.**

## Answer to "will shops want our solution"

Do not reason about this. **Test it in three weeks, in Hyderabad, for free.**

Visit 30 businesses in each shortlisted vertical and ask exactly three questions:

1. **"What did you need last month and not get?"** — Name the SKU. If he cannot name
   one, there is no availability pain and you are a vitamin in that category.
2. **"How much of your money is sitting in stock that isn't moving?"** — This tells you
   whether you are relieving a pain or adding to one.
3. **"If I delivered it tomorrow at the same price, would you pay cash?"** — The
   urgency test. Enthusiasm that evaporates at "pay cash" was never demand.

Then one closing question, which is the real one:

> **"Who do you buy this from today, and what is wrong with them?"**

If the answer is "nothing, he's fine" — walk away from that category. You cannot
build a painkiller against a supplier who is not hurting anyone.

Thirty conversations per vertical, three verticals, three weeks, two people. That
settles the question far better than any report, including this one.

## What I would do

**Test auto and artificial jewellery in parallel, in Hyderabad, in three weeks.** They
are the only two on your list that combine a real pain with a TAM worth the next eight
years of your life. Drop apparel now, and drop pet unless the field work surprises you.

Then commit to one and do not revisit. Per the previous memo, you have one pivot left.
