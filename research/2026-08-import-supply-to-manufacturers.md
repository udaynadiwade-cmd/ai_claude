# Importing for the manufacturing sector — evaluation

**Date:** August 2026 · **Status:** evaluation, fresh search
**Question:** can Befach regularly supply imported inputs to Indian manufacturers?

---

## Short answer

**This is the strongest idea put forward in this whole series.** It fixes the specific
weakness that broke the earlier plan. But one of the three reasons given for liking it
is wrong, and that wrong reason is about money.

---

## Where the thinking is right

### 1. Repeat purchase — correct, and this is the big one

Manufacturing inputs get consumed and reordered. The same factory buys the same
component again and again.

**Why this matters more than it looks:** the earlier three-category model had one fatal
gap — to launch an own brand we need many customers buying the *same* product, but a
catalog of millions of random items attracts one-off buyers. **Manufacturing solves
this by design.** A factory does not order randomly. It orders the same thing on a
cycle.

This is the difference between a business that can reach the own-brand stage and one
that cannot.

### 2. Import dependence is real, large and documented

| Fact | Figure |
|---|---|
| Electronics imports as share of total supply | **29.8%** — one of India's three most import-intensive sectors (Crisil, Aug 2026) `FACT` |
| Electronic integrated circuits imported, FY26 | **$30 billion** `FACT` |
| Semiconductors imported | $4.9 billion `FACT` |
| Electric accumulators | $4.9 billion `FACT` |
| Electric circuit apparatus | $2.1 billion `FACT` |
| Total imports from China, FY26 | **$131.63 billion**, trade deficit above $112 billion `FACT` |
| APIs, bulk drugs and intermediates imported FY25 | $4.35 billion, **China supplies 73.7%** `FACT` |

India still imports advanced display panels, memory chips, image sensors and many
specialised components. `FACT`

### 3. The pain is named by outsiders, not just by us

KPMG India (May 2026) lists what holds back Indian electronics manufacturing:
**"import dependence, higher costs, fragmented supplier networks, limited Tier-2 and
Tier-3 suppliers, and the absence of large integrated clusters."** `FACT`

"Fragmented supplier networks" and "limited Tier-2 and Tier-3 suppliers" is the
opportunity stated in someone else's words. That is stronger evidence than us
asserting it.

### 4. No dominant platform found in this exact niche

Searching for who supplies imported industrial inputs to Indian manufacturers returns
**scattered sourcing agents** — Apogee Sourcing, Swades, Indian Sourcing Agent, and
many small trading firms. Most are actually India→world (helping foreigners buy Indian
goods), not world→India for Indian factories.

`ESTIMATE` No funded platform found owning this space, unlike Medikabazaar in medical
supplies or Romegamart in water. **This is the first category screened in this series
where the named-competitor check did not turn up a dominant incumbent.**

The real competition is not a platform. It is: large manufacturers' own import
departments, traditional offline import agents, and direct factory relationships.

## Where the thinking is wrong

### The payment assumption is the weak link

**The belief:** large companies, structured payments, so payment is not a problem.

**What the evidence says:**
- Standard Indian B2B payment terms are **30, 45, 60 or 90 days** `FACT`
- In textiles, **90 days is accepted normal practice** `FACT`
- "Structured" means predictable. It does not mean fast.

**The cash problem, stated plainly:**
- We pay the Chinese factory upfront, or open a letter of credit
- Goods take 4–8 weeks on water
- Customer pays us 45–60 days after delivery
- **That is roughly 3–4 months of our money tied up in every order**

**This is credit.** We would be lending to our customers, just calling it working
capital. That must be said openly, because avoiding credit was an original goal.

### The bigger structural problem: who actually needs us

- **Large manufacturers already import.** They have import departments, existing
  Chinese suppliers, often direct factory relationships. They do not need Befach.
- **Small and mid manufacturers need help** — no import team, no China contacts, no
  customs knowledge.
- **But small and mid manufacturers are exactly the ones with weaker cash.**

**So the tension is: the ones who can pay do not need us. The ones who need us pay
slower.**

---

## One legal finding that genuinely helps

**India's MSME 45-day payment rule.** `FACT`

- A buyer must pay a registered MSME supplier within **45 days** of accepting goods
- If there is no agreed date, within **15 days**
- The statute **overrides** longer terms written into a purchase order — a 60 or 90 day
  PO does not survive it
- Buyers who miss it lose the tax deduction on that expense

**Why this matters for us:** if Befach is MSME-registered and sells to larger
manufacturers, the law is on our side on payment timing in a way it is not for a
normal trading company. It does not eliminate the cash gap, but it caps it and gives
real leverage in a dispute.

`ESTIMATE` This should be confirmed with a CA for our specific case before being relied
on — the rule's exact application depends on registration status and buyer type.

## Scores

| | Score | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Ability to execute** | **8/10** | This is the business we already run. 100 clients, 100–150 shipments, customs and freight known. Only the customer type narrows. |
| **Profitability** | **7/10** | Import margin beats domestic distribution margin. Repeat orders lower selling cost. But working capital eats into it. |
| **Scalability** | **7/10** | Each customer buys more over time. But growth needs cash for every new order, so it scales with capital, not for free. |

Higher than the three-category catalog model on execution and profit. Slightly lower on
scalability, because the free catalog scaled at no cost and this does not.

---

## What to do first — GTM

1. **Go to the existing ~100 clients and separate them.** Which are manufacturers?
   Which have reordered the same item more than once? That list is the starting point,
   and it costs nothing to make.
2. **Pick one component category** where we already know the duty structure and the
   compliance rules. Not many. One.
3. **Target mid-size manufacturers in and around Hyderabad first** — Jeedimetla,
   Balanagar, Patancheru–Bollaram, Nacharam. Large enough to have real volume, small
   enough to lack an import department.
4. **The pitch is not price. It is reliability of supply** — the same part, on the same
   cycle, without them running an import desk.
5. **Register as MSME before selling**, so the 45-day rule applies from the first order.

---

## The honest risks

1. **Working capital is the whole game.** Every order ties up cash for 3–4 months.
   Growth is limited by how much money we can put in, not by demand.
2. **Compliance per component.** BIS and QCO rules differ by product. This is why the
   category must be narrow at the start.
3. **Customers can go direct.** Once a factory knows the Chinese supplier, they can
   deal with them. Our protection is the hassle of running imports — real, but it
   weakens as the customer grows.
4. **China concentration risk.** Any tariff change, port issue or policy shift hits
   the whole book at once.
