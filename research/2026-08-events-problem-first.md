# Events & weddings — starting from the problem, not the mechanism

**Date:** August 2026 · **Status:** reframe, per founder instruction
**Prompted by:** "don't get locked to pooling — think independently about the problem
and opportunity"

## Correction to my own approach

The muhurat-demand-correlation finding from the last note is still true and still
worth knowing. But I made an error in how I used it: **I found one mechanism didn't
fit, and concluded the category should be folded into an existing answer (jewellery)
rather than asking what problem *does* fit it.** That's optimising for a clean
narrative, not for the best answer. Redone properly, working from the pain outward.

## The actual pains in this ecosystem, unranked

### 1. Sourcing is scattered across specialist regional clusters with no aggregator

Decor supply in India runs through named, separate hubs: **Moradabad for brassware
and metal decor, Surat/Gujarat for drapes and fabric backdrops, Jaipur for embroidered
tapestries and props.** `FACT` A decorator assembling one event's look is dealing with
three or more disconnected regional supply chains, each with its own minimum order,
lead time and freight arrangement — and nothing in the public record suggests anyone
has consolidated this into a single sourcing relationship.

**This is a fragmented-supply problem, not a fragmented-demand problem** — a different
shape from everything else screened in this series. Bearings and electricals are about
many *buyers* each holding too much; this is about one buyer facing too many
*disconnected sellers*.

### 2. Vendors are financing their customers, unwillingly, at scale

> *"Decoration vendors often need to pay suppliers in advance before clients settle —
> paying suppliers in 30 days while clients take 60 days silently drains cash."*
> `FACT`

This is not a hypothesis — it's documented enough that fintech products already exist
around it: *"credit-linked wallets... allowing decoration vendors to receive advance
payments before clients settle, through micro-credit via fintech partners."* `FACT`
Couples themselves reach for personal loans partly to cover vendor advance demands.
`FACT`

The pattern is exact: a small vendor pays a supplier or a hall or a caterer's raw
material bill upfront, and collects from the couple's family on a staggered,
often-delayed schedule. **The vendor is an unwilling, unpriced lender to a family that
is itself often financing the wedding.** That gap is real money sitting stuck at the
worst possible point in a fragmented, cash-tight trade.

### 3. Individual vendor capacity utilisation is not as correlated as the macro calendar suggests

The correction to my own earlier note: **the finding that all weddings cluster onto ~52
national dates is true at the level of "which dates are auspicious." It does not mean
every individual decorator, caterer or tent house in a city is booked on every one of
those dates.** A city has hundreds of vendors and far more standing capacity than any
single date requires on average — vendors simply have no visibility into each other's
slack. One decorator fully booked on a date; another ten minutes away, sitting idle the
same day, with no way to know it or route the overflow. **That is a capacity-matching
problem, not an inventory problem**, and correlated demand does not defeat it the way
it defeats pooling.

### 4. Return gifts and trousseau items are a real bulk-purchase line, bought by the wrong customer to be B2B

Wedding favours and return gifts are bought in volume, close to a fixed date, fairly
standardised (boxes, small gift items, sweets packaging). But the buyer is usually the
family — a one-time purchase, which fails the "acquire once, sell forever" requirement.
**The fix is who you sell to, not what you sell:** the same line sold wholesale to
professional event planners, who buy it repeatedly across many clients' weddings,
restores the repeat-customer shape.

### 5. Quantity and quality estimation waste — real, but not a commerce problem

Indian catering culture treats running short of food as a serious social failure, so
caterers systematically over-order, and wastage is high. This is real pain, but it is
a *forecasting and planning* problem, closer to a software product than to anything
sold as goods. Noted, not pursued.

### 6. Vendor discovery and trust — already served

Two-thirds of spend still runs through unformalised family vendors, and that is a
genuine trust gap for couples. But it is exactly the problem WedMeGood, ShaadiSaga and
similar platforms already work on, and it is a matching/marketplace business, not a
goods business. Not a fit for the sourcing-and-distribution capability this whole
series has been built around.

## Two ideas worth taking seriously, for different reasons

### A — Become the single sourcing relationship a decorator needs

Instead of asking a decorator to deal with Moradabad, Surat and Jaipur separately,
**aggregate across all three regional clusters and sell one simplified catalogue.**
This is not inventing a new capability — it is applying the exact operating muscle
Befach already has from China sourcing, domestically, across India's own fragmented
craft-manufacturing hubs. **The mechanism is consolidation of scattered supply, not
pooling of correlated demand**, and it sidesteps the muhurat problem entirely, because
you are not trying to smooth a synchronised peak — you are trying to be the one
efficient channel through an inefficient, scattered one.

### B — Finance the vendor's working capital gap, attached to a supply relationship

The pain is documented, sizeable, and currently being addressed only by generic fintech
tools rather than anyone embedded in the supply relationship itself. This is the same
shape as the Oxyzo finding from early in this series — a lending layer wrapped around
a commerce relationship captures disproportionate margin because it is underwriting a
transaction you can already see, not a stranger's balance sheet. **It would need to
come after a real supply relationship exists**, not as a first product — but it is
worth naming now as the natural second act, the way OfBusiness sequenced it.

Both of these are genuinely different mechanisms from anything scored elsewhere in this
series. Neither depends on the pooling logic, and neither is defeated by the
correlated-demand finding — which was correct as a fact and wrong as a place to stop
thinking.

## What I would test, if this is worth a field visit

Different questions from the ones used for bearings, because the pain is different:

1. **"How many separate cities or suppliers do you source decor items from for one
   event?"** — sizes the fragmented-sourcing pain directly.
2. **"When do you pay your suppliers, and when does your client pay you?"** — sizes the
   working-capital gap in the vendor's own words.
3. **"On your busiest weeks, do you ever turn down work you could have done if you'd
   had more stock or more people?"** — tests whether capacity-matching would actually
   move business, or whether vendors already flex their own capacity informally.
4. **"If one supplier could give you everything — metal decor, fabric, props — in one
   order, what would that be worth to you?"** — prices the aggregation idea directly.

These are three or four extra conversations, addable to whichever field trip runs
first, not a separate initiative.
