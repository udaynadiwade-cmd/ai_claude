# Inventory pooling in retail — why it is a different, harder business

**Date:** August 2026 · **Status:** evaluation
**Question:** the same pooling model, but for the retail consumer industry.

---

## The short answer

**The pooling model works in industrial B2B and structurally struggles in retail — and
there is a $1.9 billion bankruptcy that proves it.**

Not once. Twice.

---

## American Tire Distributors — the retail pooling model, run at maximum scale, twice bankrupt

ATD was the **largest replacement tyre distributor in North America** — 122 distribution
centres in the US and 24 in Canada, holding pooled tyre inventory and delivering to
fragmented independent dealers so those dealers would not have to hold it themselves.
`FACT`

That is your model, in retail, executed at national scale by a company with every
advantage.

It filed **Chapter 11 in October 2024 — for the second time in six years** — carrying
about **$1.91 Bn of funded debt against roughly $30 million of cash**, and in March 2025
sold substantially all its assets to its own lenders. `FACT`

The stated cause is worth reading closely:

> *"Changes in the industry increased competition, depressed pricing, and accelerated
> the shift to omnichannel retail, making ATD's legacy distribution model increasingly
> vulnerable."* `FACT`

Tyres are, on paper, the **best** retail pooling category available: perfectly
standardised sizing, a brutal SKU matrix, no meaningful obsolescence, a customer who
cannot wait, and a hugely fragmented dealer base. If it fails there, the problem is not
the category.

---

## Why industrial pools and retail does not

The difference is one number, and it is roughly a hundredfold.

| | Industrial | Retail |
|---|---|---|
| Who holds the stock | Factory | Dealer |
| **What a stockout costs them** | **Production stops — ₹20,000–1,00,000 per hour** | **One lost sale — ₹500–1,500 of margin** |
| Willingness to pay for availability | High, and quantified | Low, and negotiable |
| Can the end customer bypass the channel? | Rarely | **Yes — online, and increasingly** |
| Is the customer base growing? | Yes | **Often shrinking, as chains and e-commerce take share** |

**A plant manager can tell you exactly what an hour of downtime costs. A shopkeeper
cannot tell you what a walked customer costs, because he assumes they will come back.**
That single asymmetry is the whole difference — pooling is a working-capital service,
and you can only charge for it in proportion to the pain it removes.

Then the second problem compounds it: **in retail you would be relieving the working
capital of a customer who is himself losing share.** ATD's dealers were being squeezed
by chains and online sellers at the same time ATD was trying to charge them for
inventory relief.

## The retail version of your idea already exists. It is called quick commerce.

This is worth sitting with.

**Blinkit is retail inventory pooling.** The dark store is a pooled warehouse; the
household stops holding a pantry; demand uncertainty across thousands of homes is
pooled into one location. It is precisely the same mechanism you described, applied to
consumer retail.

And you already know its economics from the earlier note: **₹14,386 Cr of quarterly
NOV across 2,243 dark stores, contribution of −₹10 to +₹15 per order, and ₹37 Cr of
adjusted EBITDA — 0.26%.** `FACT` First profitable player in the category, after years
and enormous capital.

Set that beside **Reliance Steel at 29.7% gross margin** doing the industrial version.
`FACT`

**Same mechanism. Two orders of magnitude apart in economics.** The variable is not
the model — it is whether the customer has a balance sheet that hurts.

---

## What actually works in retail instead: postponement

Where retail has genuinely solved the inventory-variety problem, it has not used
pooling. It has used **postponement** — hold the *undifferentiated input* at the point
of sale and create the variant on demand.

**The proof is in every paint shop in India.** A dealer once had to stock hundreds of
finished shades. Now he holds around ten bases and a tinting machine, and mixes the
shade while the customer waits. Inventory collapsed, availability went to effectively
100%, and **nobody needed a distributor to hold anything.**

The same principle, applied elsewhere:

| Category | Undifferentiated input held | Variant created on demand | Status in India |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paint | Bases + colourants | Any shade, in-store | **Solved** |
| Steel | Coil, plate | Cut to size | The service-centre model |
| Optical | Lens blanks | Ground to prescription | Partly — labs exist |
| Jewellery | Gold metal | Design made to order | Solved via gold metal loans |
| Glass, mirror | Sheets | Cut to size | Fragmented, unorganised |
| Laminates, ply | Full boards | Cut to size | Fragmented |

**Where postponement is possible it beats pooling outright**, because it removes the
delivery-speed problem entirely — there is nothing to deliver, and no four-hour promise
to keep.

## If you still want a retail-facing version, here is the honest ranking

Note first: **every workable answer is B2B serving a retail end market, not retail
itself.** That is not a coincidence. Pooling relieves a balance sheet, and only a
business has one.

| Candidate | Case for | Case against | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Optical lenses to opticians** | Power matrix (sphere × cylinder × axis × index × coating) is combinatorially impossible to stock; genuinely pooled by labs already; tiny, cheap to move; zero obsolescence | Customers already accept a 3–7 day wait, so **stockout costs almost nothing** — which is exactly what kills the pricing power. Lenskart is consolidating the channel | **Best mechanics, weakest urgency** |
| **Auto spare parts to garages** | The worst SKU matrix in retail; a car on a lift cannot wait; e-commerce struggles with fitment uncertainty; garages are fragmented and not disappearing | Fitment complexity is the graveyard of this category; counterfeit liability | **Strongest — and it is B2B, which is why** |
| **Tyres to dealers** | Perfect standardisation, brutal matrix, urgent customer, fragmented base | **ATD.** India is perhaps fifteen years behind on omnichannel, so you would be building toward a known failure mode | **Avoid, on the evidence** |
| **Tiles, sanitaryware to dealers** | High display inventory, standardised SKUs, slow turns | Design obsolescence; customers wait for projects; Infra.Market is there | Weak |
| **Footwear, apparel to retailers** | Size matrix is the classic pooling problem | Fashion risk transfers to you; that is the opposite of what you want | **No** |

---

## What I would tell you plainly

1. **Your pooling idea is good, and it is good in industrial, not retail.** The
   mechanism needs a customer whose stockout is expensive and whose channel is not being
   disintermediated. Factories qualify. Shops mostly do not.
2. **If you want the retail end market, reach it through a business customer** — garages
   rather than car owners, opticians rather than spectacle wearers. The pooling logic
   survives; the retail exposure comes free.
3. **Where you see a variety problem in retail, ask whether postponement solves it
   before pooling does.** If the variant can be created at the point of sale, no
   distributor is needed and the opportunity is a machine, not a warehouse.
4. **The bearings conclusion stands.** Nothing in retail beats it on the mechanism, and
   the ten factory visits remain the cheapest, fastest test available to you.
