The muhurat problem

A $139 billion market with two-thirds still running through unformalised family vendors — and a demand pattern that breaks the pooling mechanism this whole series has been built on.

Bottom line

Not a pooling business. A genuinely good seasonal distribution business, closest in shape to artificial jewellery — and it should probably be considered alongside jewellery rather than as a rival to bearings.

India's wedding services market is $139.33 Bn in 2026, growing at 15.32% CAGR, with over two-thirds of spend still running through family-run decorators and caterers. Fact That is real, and it is bigger than every category screened so far except electricals and auto.

But weddings do not fail bearings' way — they fail a completely different way, and it is worth understanding precisely, because the same flaw would sink any "events" business built on the pooling logic that worked so well elsewhere in this series.

Why the pooling model specifically breaks here

The entire mechanism behind the bearings recommendation was risk pooling: when many customers' demand is independent and randomly timed, one shared inventory pool needs far less safety stock than the sum of what each customer would hold alone. That is why 25 factories pooling bearing stock could run at roughly a fifth of their combined individual holding.

Wedding demand is not independent. It is the opposite.

India sees roughly 10 million weddings a year, concentrated almost entirely into two windows — November through February, and April through June. Fact Within 2026 specifically, there are only 52 auspicious muhurat dates across the whole year, and four entire months — January, August, September, October — have zero. Fact Akshaya Tritiya alone is described as the single busiest wedding day of the year nationally. Fact

This means thousands of decorators, caterers and tent houses across a city all need the same fabric, the same lighting rigs, the same chafing dishes, on the same handful of dates. There is no independent, randomly-timed demand to pool — every customer's peak is everyone else's peak, simultaneously. Pooling inventory across correlated demand does not reduce the safety stock needed; it just moves the same peak onto your books instead of theirs.

Why the bearings maths does not transfer
Bearings — demand independent across 25 factories:
Pooled requirement ≈ sum × 1/√25 ≈ 20% of individual total

Wedding decor — demand correlated across N decorators on the same muhurat date:
Pooled requirement ≈ sum × ~100% of individual total, on that date
— pooling saves nothing when everyone needs it at once

What that does and does not rule out

  • It rules out a "hold central inventory, deliver on demand" model as a capital-efficiency play — you cannot run this the way the bearings business runs, because the peak cannot be smoothed away by aggregation.
  • It does not rule out ordinary distribution. Muhurat dates are known years in advance from the panchang. Vendors book events months ahead and can order supplies weeks ahead — this is not a same-day emergency category the way a snapped bearing or a tripped MCB is. The business this actually resembles is seasonal FMCG distribution — stock up ahead of a known peak, sell through it, hold light through the trough. Diwali goods distributors, school-supply distributors and agri-input distributors all run this exact shape successfully in India.
  • It rewards forecasting over pooling — which is a different skill, not a lesser one, and still squarely inside a Six Sigma toolkit: demand planning against a known calendar rather than statistical safety stock against random demand.

Where the real opportunity sits

Not with the operators of weddings — with the businesses that supply them, and specifically with the consumable inputs rather than the capital equipment.

Why this connects directly to the jewellery candidate already on the shortlist

Artificial jewellery and wedding decor are not two separate ideas — they are the same customer occasion, two product lines. Indian weddings drive enormous bridal and gifting jewellery demand, and the imitation jewellery trade already prices around the wedding calendar. A business built around supplying the Indian wedding season — bridal and party jewellery plus decor consumables, both light, both import-heavy, both sold to fragmented small vendors — is a more coherent single business than either category alone, and it uses the founder's sourcing and branding strengths on both product lines at once.

What I would do

  1. Do not chase the pooling angle here. It is the one category in this whole series where the mechanism itself is structurally wrong, not just risky or unproven.
  2. If you want to pursue it, treat it as a seasonal distribution business — pre-stock ahead of the November–February and April–June windows using the known muhurat calendar, and hold light through January and the monsoon months when there are no auspicious dates at all.
  3. Test it as one wedding-season supply line alongside artificial jewellery, not as a separate sixth category — same customer occasion, same import profile, same seasonal calendar. Add two or three decorator and jewellery-shop conversations to whichever field test you run first.
  4. Keep bearings and welding as the primary shortlist. This is a genuinely good addition to the resell-tier options, not a challenger to the operate-tier ones.
