The Grainger gap

The same business earns 39% gross margin in America and 16.7% in China. That single comparison answers the copy-paste question better than any market size ever will.

Bottom line

The Ola/Uber and Flipkart/Amazon analogy does not hold for B2B — and the reason matters more than the conclusion.

Consumer models transplant because consumer wants are similar everywhere. **B2B economics depend on what business buyers will pay a premium for, and that varies enormously with market maturity.** Grainger earns 39.1% gross margin selling MRO in America. ZKH earns 16.7% selling the same thing in China and only turned adjusted-profitable this year, twenty-eight years after founding. Fact

So do not copy a company. Copy a mechanism. Two are worth copying and neither exists in India: Fastenal's onsite vending and McMaster-Carr's guaranteed-availability, no-negotiation catalogue. One is worth avoiding, and India is already learning that lesson expensively.

Why the gap exists

Grainger does not sell products. It sells uptime. An American factory's maintenance engineer costs more per hour than the premium Grainger charges, so paying 39% over the commodity price to have the part today is rational arithmetic, not loyalty.

In China — and in India — the buyer is price-driven, labour is cheaper relative to goods, and the informal alternative (the local market, the trade contact, the man who can get it by evening) is cheap and adequate. **The willingness to pay for availability is a function of what an hour of the buyer's time is worth.**

India sits closer to China than to the US on that axis today, and will move slowly. Any Indian plan modelled on Grainger's margins is modelling the wrong country.

The clearest lesson in this document

The emerging-market B2B retail marketplace has failed on three continents. Africa's two largest — Wasoko and MaxAB — merged in distress after retreating from four countries. India's largest lost ₹1,055 Cr in a single year.

The pattern is not bad execution. It is the model: tiny baskets, price-driven buyers, delivery cost near or above gross margin, and no reason for the retailer to stay once a cheaper option appears. If anyone pitches you the Udaan shape, this is your answer.

The two mechanisms worth copying

1 — Fastenal's onsite vending: the strongest acquire-once model in global B2B

Fastenal puts physical vending and bin-stock devices inside the customer's own plant and restocks them on a schedule. The customer does not order. The customer consumes, and the device reports.

The numbers are extraordinary: 111,800 devices generating $2.07 Bn a year — about $24,000 per device — and the strategic effect is more striking still. Fastenal cut its branch network by 36% since 2014 and grew revenue 85%, because the device replaced the branch. Fact

Read that against your own requirement — *acquire the customer once, never re-acquire.* **This is that requirement, solved, at scale, with hardware.** Once your device is bolted to a factory wall, you are not a supplier on a list. You are part of their process, and removing you costs them a project.

Nobody runs this model in India. Moglix and ProcMart sell procurement; neither has put the physical device on the customer's floor at scale. `ASSUMPTION — verify before building on it`

2 — McMaster-Carr: sell certainty, refuse to negotiate

McMaster's proposition is that everything in the catalogue is in stock and ships today, at one published price, with no negotiation. Engineers use it not because it is cheap — it is not — but because it removes a decision and a delay.

In India this is close to unthinkable: **every trade transaction is negotiated, and no distributor guarantees availability.** Which is precisely why it is interesting. A supplier who publishes one price and always has stock would be doing something structurally novel — and would attract exactly the customers who value their own time.

It is also the same thesis as your quick-commerce idea, arrived at from a different direction. McMaster sells certainty over a day; you would be selling it over forty-five minutes.

But apply the Grainger gap to both

These mechanisms work in markets where buyers pay for certainty. **The open question for India is not whether you can build them — it is whether anyone will pay the premium.** ZKH's 16.7% says the premium is thin in emerging markets.

Which makes one field-test question more important than any other: "What does an hour of waiting actually cost you?" If your electricians and contractors can answer that with a number, the certainty premium exists and these models work. If they shrug, you are selling a vitamin in a market that only pays for painkillers.

So what does transplant?

  • Not the company. Grainger's margins are a function of American labour costs, not of Grainger's cleverness.
  • Not the marketplace. Proven failure in India, Africa and beyond.
  • Yes, the mechanism — onsite devices, guaranteed availability, published pricing, VMI. These are operating designs and they travel.
  • And yes, the discipline. Grainger's 39% and Fastenal's device economics rest on inventory science: what to stock, where, at what service level, replenished how. That is transferable anywhere, and it is the part of this that suits you specifically.

One structural observation about the Indian opportunity. Grainger's own guidance separates High-Touch Solutions (16.9–17.4% operating margin) from Endless Assortment (10.0–10.5%). Fact The service-intensive business earns roughly 60% more margin than the catalogue business.

Every Indian B2B startup has built the endless-assortment version — the app, the SKU count, the marketplace. **Almost nobody has built the high-touch version**, which is the one that actually earns. That is the gap, and it is wider in India than in the US because the competition is entirely price-based.

What I would take from this into your decision

  1. Stop benchmarking against Udaan. Benchmark against Fastenal. One lost ₹1,055 Cr in a year; the other shrank its footprint by a third and grew 85%.
  2. Model your business at ZKH's margins, not Grainger's. Assume 15–18% gross, not 39%. If the plan only works at 30%+, it does not work.
  3. Test the certainty premium explicitly in the field. "What does an hour of waiting cost you?" is now the most important of the five questions.
  4. Consider the onsite device as a phase-two weapon, not a phase-one product. Once you have a route and know what a customer consumes, a physical stocked cabinet at their premises is the strongest lock-in available — and it is unclaimed in India.
